## Executive Summary

This monograph situates the `GLC-AOM` / `GLCSearchTransformer` (`v1`, `v2`, and `v3` horizon) architecture within fifty years of search literature spanning three foundational traditions:
1. **Automated Algorithm Selection, Tabu Metaheuristics, and Hyper-Heuristics:** From Rice's (1976) Algorithm Selection Problem and Xu et al.'s (2008) `SATzilla` portfolio selectors to Glover's (1986, 1989) Tabu Search memory structures and Burke et al.'s (2003, 2013, 2019) selection/generation hyper-heuristics. We prove that while classical portfolio selectors (`SATzilla`, `Portfolio-Oracle` in `R14`) dispatch one monolithic algorithm per problem instance $x$, `GLCSearch-v2` operates as an **in-context, per-node continuous hyper-heuristic** that dynamically synthesizes and blends CDCL nogood pruning, MCTS/MCGS subtree averaging, alternating Minimax/PNS backup, Disjunctive Bayes marginalization, CFR+ infoset pooling, and Gumbel Sequential Halving *inside the same search tree*. Furthermore, we establish an exact mathematical correspondence between Glover's short-term recency memory, aspiration criteria, and long-term frequency diversification and `GLC-AOM`'s discrete action-exhaustion mask $\mathrm{Used}(j)$, continuous **Ridge-Damped Gram Orthogonal Projection** $P_\perp(K_{\text{done}})$, Dual-Channel Witness Override, and **Gershgorin Row-Crowding Preconditioner** $D_{\text{crowd}, r}$.
2. **Nested Monte-Carlo Search (`NMCS`), `NRPA` / `GNRPA`, and Amortized Fast-Weight Plan Conditioning:** From Cazenave's (2009) `NMCS` and Rosin's (2011) `NRPA` to Cazenave's (2020) `GNRPA`. We analyze why exact `NRPA`—which updates an episodic fast-weight policy table $w_{\text{fast}}(a)$ via online policy-gradient ascent toward the best trajectory at nesting level $\ell - 1$—forms `Society Round 4`'s `V3.4` expressivity barrier for fixed-weight test-time Transformers, and how `GLC-AOM`'s **Mixture-of-Power-Spherical (`MoPS`) Fractal Prospectus Plan** $p_r \in \mathbb{S}^{D_{\text{amp}}-1}$, **Orthogonal Residual Plan Transport**, and **Hindsight Plan Conditioning** act as a continuous, neural, amortized counterpart to `GNRPA`'s temperature-and-bias policy adaptation.
3. **Modern Game-Tree and Graph Search (`UCT`, `RAVE`/`GRAVE`, `MCGS`, `PNS`/`df-pn`, and `Gumbel MuZero`):** We trace the evolution of tree and DAG search from Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006) and Coulom (2006) through Gelly & Silver's (2007, 2011) `RAVE`, Cazenave's (2015) `GRAVE`, Saffidine et al.'s (2012) and Czech et al.'s (2020, 2021) `MCGS`, Allis et al.'s (1994) `PNS` and Nagai's (2002) `df-pn`, and Danihelka et al.'s (2022) `Gumbel MuZero` (built on Karnin et al.'s 2013 Sequential Halving). For each paradigm, we derive the exact classical equations, diagnose the structural failure mode of naive tree Transformers (`Theorem R4.1`–`R4.3`), and prove how `GLCSearch-v2`'s relational operator basis (`V2.1'`–`V2.5'`) and `v3` extensions (`V3.1`–`V3.4`) resolve them.

## 1. Metaheuristics, Hyper-Heuristics & The Algorithm Selection Problem

### 1.1 Rice (1976), `SATzilla` (Xu et al., 2008), and Automated Algorithm Selection (Kerschke et al., 2019)

#### 1.1.1 Rice's (1976) Abstract Model of the Algorithm Selection Problem
In his foundational treatise *"The Algorithm Selection Problem"* (*Advances in Computers*, Vol. 15, pp. 65–118, 1976), John R. Rice formalized the observation that no single search or numerical algorithm dominates across all problem instances. Rice defined a four-space mathematical architecture $(\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{F}, \mathcal{Y})$:
- **Problem Space $\mathcal{P}$:** A set (or probability space $(\mathcal{P}, \Sigma, \mathbb{P})$) of problem instances $x \in \mathcal{P}$ (e.g., CSP/SAT formulas, puzzle boards, or extensive-form game states).
- **Algorithm Space (Portfolio) $\mathcal{A}$:** A finite or parameterized family of candidate algorithms $A \in \mathcal{A}$.
- **Feature Space $\mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{d_f}$:** A measurable space of instance characteristics extracted by a computationally cheap feature map $f: \mathcal{P} \to \mathcal{F}$, $x \mapsto f(x)$.
- **Performance Mapping $y: \mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{P} \to \mathcal{Y} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$:** A functional measuring the quality or negative cost of executing algorithm $A$ on instance $x$ under a fixed resource budget $B$ (e.g., negative KL divergence $-\mathrm{KL}(P^*(V \mid x) \Vert \mu_B^A(V))$, verified witness discovery $\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{Check}(x, \hat{w}_B^A)=1]$, or game-theoretic payoff).

Rice's **Algorithm Selection Problem (ASP)** seeks a selection mapping $S: \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{A}$ that maximizes expected performance across $\mathcal{P}$:
$$S^* \;\in\; \arg\max_{S: \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{P}}\big[ y(S(f(x)), \, x) \big].$$

In modern Automated Algorithm Selection (AAS), **Kerschke, Hoos, Neumann, & Trautmann (2019)** (*"Automated Algorithm Selection: Survey and Perspectives"*, *Evolutionary Computation*, Vol. 27(1), pp. 3–45) benchmark any selector $S$ against two canonical baselines:
1. **Single Best Solver (SBS):** The single fixed algorithm that maximizes mean performance across the distribution:
   $$A_{\text{SBS}} \;=\; \arg\max_{A \in \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{P}}\big[ y(A, x) \big].$$
2. **Virtual Best Solver (VBS / Portfolio Oracle):** The clairvoyant per-instance oracle that selects the best algorithm in hindsight for each instance $x$:
   $$A_{\text{VBS}}(x) \;=\; \arg\max_{A \in \mathcal{A}} y(A, x), \qquad y_{\text{VBS}} \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{P}}\Big[ \max_{A \in \mathcal{A}} y(A, x) \Big].$$

The fraction of the VBS–SBS gap closed by selector $S$ is:
$$\mathrm{GapClosed}(S) \;=\; \frac{\mathbb{E}_x[y(S(f(x)), x)] - \mathbb{E}_x[y(A_{\text{SBS}}, x)]}{\mathbb{E}_x[\max_{A \in \mathcal{A}} y(A, x)] - \mathbb{E}_x[y(A_{\text{SBS}}, x)]} \;\le\; 1.$$
Crucially, under Rice's static per-instance paradigm, **$\mathrm{GapClosed}(S)$ is upper-bounded by $1.0$**: a pure selector $S: \mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{A}$ can never outperform the hindsight Portfolio Oracle $\max_{A \in \mathcal{A}} y(A, x)$ on any instance $x$, because it must commit to a single monolithic algorithm $A \in \mathcal{A}$ for the entire execution on $x$.

#### 1.1.2 `SATzilla` (Xu, Hutter, Hoos, & Leyton-Brown, 2008)
**Xu, Hutter, Hoos, & Leyton-Brown (2008)** (*"SATzilla: Portfolio-based Algorithm Selection for SAT"*, *JAIR*, Vol. 32, pp. 565–606) operationalized Rice's framework for NP-complete Boolean Satisfiability via **Empirical Hardness Models (EHMs)**:
1. **Instance Feature Extraction ($f_{\text{SAT}}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{48}$):** Combines static syntactic features (variable-to-clause ratio $c/v$, Horn clause fraction, positive/negative literal balance, variable-clause graph node degree statistics) with **cheap short-horizon probing features** (fraction of variables unit-propagated by 2-step DPLL lookahead; local search basin depth after 500 GSAT/SAPS steps).
2. **Ridge-Regression Empirical Hardness Models:** For each solver $A \in \mathcal{A}$, `SATzilla` fits a quadratic basis expansion $\Phi(f(x)) \in \mathbb{R}^D$ to predict log-runtime (or censored PAR-10 score) via ridge regression:
   $$\hat{c}_A(x) \;=\; w_A^\top \Phi(f(x)), \qquad w_A \;=\; \big(\Phi^\top \Phi + \lambda_{\text{ridge}} I_D\big)^{-1} \Phi^\top \mathbf{y}_A,$$
   and dispatches instance $x$ (if not solved during the pre-solver / probing stage) to $S_{\text{SATzilla}}(x) = \arg\min_{A \in \mathcal{A}} \hat{c}_A(x)$.
3. **Kerschke et al. (2019) Synthesis:** Kerschke et al. extended this paradigm to continuous and combinatorial optimization via Exploratory Landscape Analysis (ELA: convexity, ruggedness, multimodality, information content), random-forest/pairwise-classification selectors (`Autofolio`, Lindauer et al., 2015), and cost-sensitive hierarchical loss functions. However, Kerschke et al. (2019, §6) highlighted a major open frontier: **per-instance static selection fails when the optimal search strategy shifts dynamically within a single solve** (e.g., transitioning from global constraint propagation to game-tree minimax refutation or multi-witness Bayesian integration).

### 1.2 Glover (1986, 1989, 1990) Tabu Search vs. `GLC-AOM`'s `K_done` Gram Projection and `Used(j)` Masks

In *"Future Paths for Integer Programming and Links to Artificial Intelligence"* (*Computers & Operations Research*, Vol. 13(5), pp. 533–549, 1986) and *"Tabu Search—Part I & Part II"* (*ORSA Journal on Computing*, Vol. 1(3), pp. 190–206, 1989; Vol. 2(1), pp. 4–32, 1990), **Fred Glover** coined the term *meta-heuristic* and introduced **Tabu Search (TS)** as a deterministic/adaptive memory architecture overlaid on local or tree search.

Glover organized Tabu Search around three interacting memory structures:
1. **Short-Term Recency Memory (Tabu List & Tenure $\tau_{\text{tabu}}$):**
   When a search step applies move attribute $m \in \mathcal{M}$ at iteration $t$, its inverse or repetition is recorded with recency timestamp $\mathrm{LastUsed}(m) = t$ and forbidden for a tabu tenure $\tau_{\text{tabu}}$:
   $$\mathcal{T}_{\text{tabu}}(t) \;=\; \big\{\, m \in \mathcal{M} \;:\; t - \mathrm{LastUsed}(m) \;\le\; \tau_{\text{tabu}}(m) \,\big\}.$$
   This prevents short-cycle trapping and redundant re-exploration of recently tested transitions.
2. **Aspiration Criteria ($A(s, m)$):**
   A hard tabu restriction can over-constrain search if a forbidden attribute leads to an exceptional breakthrough. Glover introduced the **aspiration criterion** $A(s, m)$ (most commonly *aspiration by global best* or *aspiration by influence*), which overrides the tabu status if the resulting state $s' = s \oplus m$ beats the best witness $z^*$ found so far:
   $$\mathrm{Admissible}(s, m, t) \;=\; \big(m \notin \mathcal{T}_{\text{tabu}}(t)\big) \;\lor\; \big(f(s \oplus m) > z^*(t)\big).$$
3. **Long-Term Frequency Memory (Diversification & Intensification):**
   Over a longer horizon $t \in \{1, \dots, b\}$, Glover maintained transition and residence frequencies $\mathrm{freq}(m) = \sum_{r=1}^b \mathbf{1}[m \in s_r]$ and augmented the move evaluation function with a frequency crowding penalty (for **diversification** into unexplored regions) or elite-attribute reinforcement (for **intensification** around promising partial solutions):
   $$E_{\text{TS}}(s \oplus m) \;=\; f(s \oplus m) \;-\; \lambda_{\text{div}} \sum_{i \in \mathrm{attr}(s \oplus m)} \mathrm{freq}(i) \;+\; \lambda_{\text{int}} \sum_{i \in \mathrm{attr}(s \oplus m)} \mathrm{elite}(i).$$

### 1.3 Burke et al. (2003, 2013, 2019) Hyper-Heuristics & GP-Evolved Tree Search (Cazenave 2007; Browne et al. 2012)

#### 1.3.1 The Burke et al. (2003, 2013, 2019) Taxonomy of Hyper-Heuristics
**Burke, Kendall, Newall, Hart, Ross, & Schulenburg (2003)** (*"Hyper-Heuristics: An Emerging Direction in Modern Search Technology"*, *Handbook of Metaheuristics*, pp. 457–474), **Burke, Gendreau, Hyde, Kendall, Ochoa, Özcan, & Qu (2013)** (*"Hyper-heuristics: a survey of the state of the art"*, *JORS*, Vol. 64(12), pp. 1695–1724), and **Burke, Hyde, Kendall, Ochoa, Özcan, & Woodward (2019)** (*"A Classification of Hyper-Heuristic Approaches: Revisited"*, *Handbook of Metaheuristics*, 3rd ed., pp. 453–477) define a **hyper-heuristic** as *"a search method or learning mechanism for selecting or generating heuristics to solve computational search problems."*

Crucially, whereas metaheuristics search directly over the **problem solution space** $\mathcal{S}$, hyper-heuristics search over a **heuristic/operator space** $\mathcal{H}$ separated from the domain state by a **domain barrier** (passing only domain-independent fitness/entropy/topology statistics across the barrier). Burke et al. classify hyper-heuristics along two orthogonal axes:
1. **Nature of the Heuristic Space:**
   - **Selection Hyper-Heuristics:** Given a fixed finite set of low-level perturbative or constructive heuristics $\mathcal{H} = \{h_1, \dots, h_K\}$, a high-level controller selects $h_{t} = \pi_{\text{HH}}(z_t) \in \mathcal{H}$ at step $t$ and applies an acceptance criterion (e.g., all-moves, improving-only, simulated annealing, or Great Deluge) to the resulting state transition.
   - **Generation Hyper-Heuristics:** Given a terminal set of atomic search statistics $\Sigma_{\text{term}}$ (e.g., node value $Q_j$, visit count $N_j$, parent visits $N_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}$, prior $P_j$, depth $d_j$, conflict count, proof/disproof numbers) and a function set of algebraic/relational operators $\Sigma_{\text{func}} = \{+, -, \times, \div, \sqrt{\cdot}, \log, \max, \min\}$, the system synthesizes a composite heuristic formula $h_\theta: \Sigma_{\text{term}} \to \mathbb{R}$ (historically via Genetic Programming).
2. **Source of Learning Feedback:**
   - **Offline Learning:** Training the hyper-heuristic parameters $\theta$ on a training set of instances before locking $\theta$ at evaluation time.
   - **Online Learning:** Adapting heuristic selection weights or operator parameters dynamically during the solve of a single test instance.

#### 1.3.2 Evolving Tree-Search Priority Formulas (Cazenave 2007; Browne et al. 2012)
In the game-tree search literature, **Cazenave (2007)** (*"Evolving Monte-Carlo Tree Search"*, Technical Report, Université Paris 8) and **Browne et al. (2012)** (*"A Survey of Monte Carlo Tree Search Methods"*, *IEEE Trans. Computational Intelligence and AI in Games*, Vol. 4(1), pp. 1–43, §IV.A & §VII) pioneered the application of **Generation Hyper-Heuristics** to MCTS node selection:
- Standard UCT selects child $j$ maximizing $Q(j) + C \sqrt{\frac{\ln N(\mathrm{pa}(j))}{N(j)}}$.
- Cazenave (2007) used Genetic Programming over expression trees built from $\{+, -, \times, \div, \sqrt{\cdot}, \ln, Q, N, N_{\text{parent}}, \text{const}\}$ to evolve novel tree-policy priority formulas evaluated by tournament win rate in Go/puzzles, discovering algebraic variants such as $Q(j) + \frac{\sqrt{\ln N(\mathrm{pa}(j)) + c_1}}{N(j) + c_2}$ that outperformed hand-tuned UCT at specific simulation budgets.
- Subsequent work in General Game Playing (GGP / `Ludii`, Browne et al. 2012; Pettit & Helmbold; Alhejali & Lucas 2013) evolved both UCT selection formulas and rollout heuristics, yet remained restricted to **scalar algebraic combinations of node-local counters** $(Q(j), N(j), N(\mathrm{pa}(j)))$ without cross-branch relational attention, infoset pooling, or global conflict-clause subsumption.

### 1.4 Direct Comparison with `R13`/`R14`: Static Portfolio Selection (`SATzilla` / `Portfolio-Oracle`) vs. `GLCSearch-v2` as an In-Context, Per-Node Continuous Hyper-Heuristic

Our Round 13 (`R13-ALGO-IMITATION`, [`benchmarks/PREREG-R13-ALGO-IMITATION.md`](../../benchmarks/PREREG-R13-ALGO-IMITATION.md)) and Round 14 (`R14-UNSUP-DISCOVERY`, [`benchmarks/PREREG-R14-UNSUP-DISCOVERY.md`](../../benchmarks/PREREG-R14-UNSUP-DISCOVERY.md)) protocols directly test Rice's (1976) / `SATzilla`'s (2008) portfolio selection paradigm against `GLCSearch-v2` (`src/search/neural_tree_controller.py`):

1. **Why `SATzilla` / `Portfolio-Oracle` Is a Coarse Per-Game Selector:**

## 2. Nested Monte-Carlo Search (`NMCS`), `NRPA` & Online Fast-Weight Adaptation

### 2.1 Cazenave (2009) `NMCS`, Rosin (2011) `NRPA`, and Cazenave (2020) `GNRPA`

#### 2.1.1 Cazenave (2009) Nested Monte-Carlo Search (`NMCS`)
In *"Nested Monte-Carlo Search"* (*Proceedings of the 21st International Joint Conference on Artificial Intelligence (IJCAI 2009)*, pp. 456–461), **Tristan Cazenave** addressed single-player deterministic combinatorial optimization and puzzle domains (Morpion Solitaire, SameGame, 16$\times$16 Sudoku, Kakuro) where UCT's averaging backup suffers from traps and high-variance rollouts.

`NMCS` defines a recursive hierarchy of search levels $\ell \in \{0, 1, \dots, L\}$:
- **Base Level ($\ell = 0$):** A randomized (or static-heuristic) rollout $\mathrm{Playout}(s)$ from state $s$ to a terminal state, returning score $R(\tau)$ and move sequence $\tau = (a_t, \dots, a_T)$.
- **Recursive Level ($\ell \ge 1$):** At each non-terminal state $s$, $\mathrm{NMCS}(s, \ell)$ maintains the best trajectory $\tau^*_{\text{best}}$ (with score $v^*_{\text{best}}$) discovered at or below $s$. For every legal action $a \in \mathcal{A}(s)$, it runs a level-$(\ell - 1)$ search $\big(v_a, \tau_a\big) \leftarrow \mathrm{NMCS}(\mathrm{Step}(s, a), \, \ell - 1)$, updates:
  $$\text{if } \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} v_a > v^*_{\text{best}}: \qquad a^* = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} v_a, \quad v^*_{\text{best}} \leftarrow v_{a^*}, \quad \tau^*_{\text{best}} \leftarrow (a^*) \circ \tau_{a^*},$$
  and then advances one step along the memorized principal variation $s \leftarrow \mathrm{Step}(s, \, \mathrm{head}(\tau^*_{\text{best}}))$, repeating until $s$ is terminal.

**Limitation of `NMCS`:** At level $\ell$, evaluating every legal move at every step of a horizon-$T$ episode requires $O(|\mathcal{A}|^\ell T^\ell)$ playouts, and the base level ($\ell = 0$) rollout policy remains completely fixed throughout the search—learning nothing about which moves worked well in earlier subtrees.

#### 2.1.2 Rosin (2011) Nested Rollout Policy Adaptation (`NRPA`)
In *"Nested Rollout Policy Adaptation for Monte Carlo Tree Search"* (*Proceedings of IJCAI 2011*, pp. 649–654), **Christopher D. Rosin** achieved world-record results on Morpion Solitaire (82 moves) and crossword puzzle generation by replacing `NMCS`'s branching over all legal actions with **online policy-gradient adaptation of a fast-weight rollout policy table** $w_{\text{fast}} \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}|}$ across nesting levels:

1. **Gibbs / Softmax Fast-Weight Rollout Policy ($\ell = 0$):**
   Each state-action transition $(s, a)$ is mapped to an integer move code $c(s, a) \in \mathcal{C}$ with fast weight $w_{\text{fast}}(c(s, a)) \in \mathbb{R}$ (initialized to $0$ at the root). At level $\ell = 0$, trajectories $\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots, s_T)$ are sampled without tree branching according to:
   $$\pi_{w_{\text{fast}}}(a \mid s) \;=\; \frac{\exp\!\big(w_{\text{fast}}(c(s, a))\big)}{\sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \exp\!\big(w_{\text{fast}}(c(s, a'))\big)}.$$
2. **Online Fast-Weight Policy-Gradient Step (`Adapt` at Level $\ell \ge 1$):**
   At level $\ell \ge 1$, `NRPA(level, w)` executes $N_{\text{iter}}$ iterations (typically $N_{\text{iter}} = 100$). In each iteration $i \in \{1, \dots, N_{\text{iter}}\}$, it passes a copy of its current fast weights $w$ down to level $\ell - 1$:
   $$(v_i, \tau_i) \;\leftarrow\; \mathrm{NRPA}(\ell - 1, \, w), \qquad \text{if } v_i \ge v^*_{\text{best}}: \quad (v^*_{\text{best}}, \tau^*_{\text{best}}) \leftarrow (v_i, \tau_i),$$
   and immediately shifts $w$ by one step of **REINFORCE / cross-entropy gradient ascent** on the log-likelihood of the best sequence $\tau^*_{\text{best}} = (s_0^*, a_0^*, \dots, s_{T-1}^*, a_{T-1}^*)$ found so far at level $\ell$:
   $$w(c(s_t^*, a)) \;\leftarrow\; w(c(s_t^*, a)) \;+\; \alpha \Big(\mathbf{1}[a = a_t^*] - \pi_{w}(a \mid s_t^*)\Big) \qquad \forall t \in \{0, \dots, T-1\}, \;\forall a \in \mathcal{A}(s_t^*),$$
   which is the exact gradient update $w \leftarrow w + \alpha \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_w \log \pi_w(a_t^* \mid s_t^*)$! Because each level $\ell$ passes its adapted fast-weight vector $w^{(\ell)}$ to level $\ell - 1$ and receives back only the best trajectory $\tau^*_{\text{best}}$ (discarding the temporary inner-level weight mutations of $\ell - 1$), `NRPA` prevents premature policy collapse while rapidly concentrating probability mass around elite move combinations.

#### 2.1.3 Cazenave (2020) Generalized NRPA (`GNRPA`)
In *"Generalized Nested Rollout Policy Adaptation"* (*Proceedings of the 34th AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence (AAAI 2020)*, Vol. 34(03), pp. 3377–3382), **Tristan Cazenave** generalized `NRPA` by separating **static/learned domain heuristic bias** $\beta_{s,a}$ from the **online-adapted fast weight** $w_{s,a}$ and introducing a **temperature parameter** $\tau > 0$:
$$\pi_{w}^{\text{GNRPA}}(a \mid s) \;=\; \frac{\exp\!\left(\frac{w_{s,a}}{\tau} + \beta_{s,a}\right)}{\sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \exp\!\left(\frac{w_{s,a'}}{\tau} + \beta_{s,a'}\right)}.$$
Differentiating $\log \pi_w^{\text{GNRPA}}(a_t^* \mid s_t^*)$ with respect to $w_{s,a}$ yields the exact `GNRPA` online adaptation rule:
$$w_{s,a} \;\leftarrow\; w_{s,a} \;+\; \frac{\alpha}{\tau}\Big(\mathbf{1}[a = a_t^*] - \pi_w^{\text{GNRPA}}(a \mid s_t^*)\Big).$$
When $\beta_{s,a} = 0$ and $\tau = 1$, `GNRPA` reduces identically to Rosin's (2011) `NRPA`; when $\beta_{s,a} = \log p_\theta(a \mid s)$ is initialized from a prior heuristic or neural network, `GNRPA` warm-starts online rollout adaptation from the prior without overwriting the static heuristic knowledge.

## 3. From UCT to `RAVE`/`GRAVE`, `MCGS` (DAGs), `df-pn`, and `Gumbel MuZero`

### 3.1 `UCT` (Kocsis & Szepesvári 2006), `Crazy Stone` (Coulom 2006), `RAVE` (Gelly & Silver 2007, 2011), and `GRAVE` (Cazenave 2015)

#### 3.1.1 Classical Formulations: `UCT`, `RAVE` (`AMAF`), and `GRAVE`
1. **Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006) `UCT` & Coulom (2006) `MCTS`:**
   **Coulom (2006)** (*"Efficient Selectivity and Backup Operators in Monte-Carlo Tree Search"*, *CG 2006*, LNCS 4630, pp. 72–83) coined *Monte-Carlo Tree Search* in his Go engine *Crazy Stone*, introducing selective tree growth and mixed minimax/mean backup operators. Concurrently, **Kocsis & Szepesvári (2006)** (*"Bandit based Monte-Carlo Planning"*, *ECML 2006*, LNCS 4212, pp. 282–293) formulated **UCT (Upper Confidence Bound applied to Trees)**, treating each interior tree node $s$ as a non-stationary multi-armed bandit (building on Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, & Fischer's 2002 `UCB1`) that selects action $a \in \mathcal{A}(s)$ maximizing:
   $$a^*_{\text{UCT}}(s) \;=\; \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \left\{\, Q(s, a) \;+\; 2 C_p \sqrt{\frac{\ln N(s)}{N(s, a)}} \,\right\}, \qquad Q(s, a) \;=\; \frac{W(s, a)}{N(s, a)}.$$
   While UCT is asymptotically consistent ($\mathbb{P}(a^*_{\text{UCT}}(s_0) = a^*_{\text{opt}}) \to 1$ as $B \to \infty$), its sample complexity scales poorly when the action branching factor $|\mathcal{A}(s)|$ is large (e.g., $361$ in Go or $V \times K$ in combinatorial boards), because each tree edge $(s, a)$ learns *only* from simulations that explicitly traverse $(s, a)$ at that exact ply.

2. **Gelly & Silver (2007, 2011) `RAVE` (Rapid Action Value Estimation / All-Moves-As-First `AMAF`):**
   Inspired by Brügmann's (1993) All-Moves-As-First (`AMAF`) heuristic in computer Go, **Gelly & Silver (2007)** (*"Combining Online and Offline Learning in UCT"*, *ICML 2007*, pp. 273–280) and **Gelly & Silver (2011)** (*"Monte-Carlo tree search and rapid action value estimation in computer Go"*, *Artificial Intelligence*, Vol. 175(11), pp. 1856–1875) introduced **RAVE**.
   In any rollout trajectory $\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots, s_T)$ passing through node $s = s_t$ with player-to-move $p = \mathrm{pl}(s_t)$, RAVE updates not only the immediate action $a_t$, but **every legal action $a \in \mathcal{A}(s_t)$ that player $p$ subsequently plays at any later step $t' \ge t$ of the same simulation** ($\mathrm{pl}(s_{t'}) = p$ and $a_{t'} = a$, provided $a$ was not played earlier at $t \le t'' < t'$):
   $$\tilde{N}_{\text{AMAF}}(s, a) \;=\; \sum_{\tau \ni s} \mathbf{1}\big[\exists t' \ge t(s): \mathrm{pl}(s_{t'}) = \mathrm{pl}(s) \land a_{t'} = a\big], \qquad \tilde{Q}_{\text{AMAF}}(s, a) \;=\; \frac{\tilde{W}_{\text{AMAF}}(s, a)}{\tilde{N}_{\text{AMAF}}(s, a)}.$$
   `MC-RAVE` interpolates between the unbiased, high-variance Monte-Carlo estimate $Q(s, a)$ and the low-variance, biased AMAF estimate $\tilde{Q}_{\text{AMAF}}(s, a)$ via a minimum-MSE schedule $\beta(s, a) \in [0, 1]$:
   $$Q_{\text{RAVE}}(s, a) \;=\; \big(1 - \beta(s, a)\big)\,Q(s, a) \;+\; \beta(s, a)\,\tilde{Q}_{\text{AMAF}}(s, a),$$
   $$\beta(s, a) \;=\; \frac{\tilde{N}(s, a)}{\tilde{N}(s, a) + N(s, a) + 4 b_{\text{bias}}^2 \, \tilde{N}(s, a)\, N(s, a)} \;\;\approx\;\; \sqrt{\frac{k_{\text{equiv}}}{3 N(s) + k_{\text{equiv}}}},$$
   where $b_{\text{bias}}^2$ represents the squared AMAF bias and $k_{\text{equiv}}$ is the equivalence parameter (number of visits at which $Q$ and $\tilde{Q}_{\text{AMAF}}$ receive equal weight $\beta = 1/2$).

3. **Cazenave (2015) `GRAVE` (Generalized Rapid Action Value Estimation):**
   In *"Generalized Rapid Action Value Estimation"* (*Proceedings of IJCAI 2015*, pp. 754–760), **Tristan Cazenave** identified a critical weakness of RAVE near the frontier of the search tree: when a node $s$ has very few visits ($N(s) \ll \mathrm{ref}$, e.g., $N(s) \in \{1, \dots, 15\}$), even its local AMAF counts $\tilde{N}_{\text{AMAF}}(s, a)$ are too sparse to provide reliable move ordering!
   `GRAVE` resolves this by **borrowing AMAF/refutation statistics from the closest ancestor $s_{\text{ref}} \in \mathrm{Anc}(s)$ whose visit count exceeds a reference threshold $\mathrm{ref}$** (typically $\mathrm{ref} \in [25, 50]$, defaulting to the root $s_0$ if no lower ancestor qualifies):
   $$s_{\text{ref}}(s) \;=\; \arg\max_{u \in \mathrm{Anc}(s)} \big\{\, d(u) \;:\; N(u) > \mathrm{ref} \;\lor\; u = s_0 \,\big\},$$
   $$Q_{\text{GRAVE}}(s, a) \;=\; \big(1 - \beta(s, a, s_{\text{ref}})\big)\,Q(s, a) \;+\; \beta(s, a, s_{\text{ref}})\,\tilde{Q}_{\text{AMAF}}\big(s_{\text{ref}}(s), \, a\big),$$
   where $\beta(s, a, s_{\text{ref}}) = \frac{\tilde{N}(s_{\text{ref}}, a)}{\tilde{N}(s_{\text{ref}}, a) + N(s, a) + 4 b_{\text{bias}}^2 \tilde{N}(s_{\text{ref}}, a) N(s, a)}$. `GRAVE` became the gold-standard baseline across General Game Playing (`GGP` and `Ludii`) because it provides rich cross-branch move-value priors at newly expanded leaves ($N(s)=1$) from step 1.

#### 3.1.2 Connection to `GLCSearch-v2` and `Society Round 4`'s `V3.1` (`Action-Identity Masks`)
In `Society Round 4` ([`society/round4/ROUND4-SCOREBOARD.md:60`](../../society/round4/ROUND4-SCOREBOARD.md#L60), [`04-opus-round4-expressivity.md:342–344, 369`](../../society/round4/04-opus-round4-expressivity.md#L342-L344)):
- **Why Pure `RAVE`/`GRAVE` Requires `V3.1`:** In `GLCSearch-v1` and `GLCSearch-v2`, the relational mask bank (`src/search/kv_tree_search.py:377–427`) keys connections by **tree/DAG topology** ($\mathcal{M}_{\text{anc}}, \mathcal{M}_{\text{desc}}, \mathcal{M}_{\text{sib}}, \mathcal{M}_{\text{desc}}^{\text{DAG}}$) or **state/infoset equivalence** ($E_{\text{trans}}, \mathcal{M}_{\text{infoset}}$). Computing exact `RAVE`/`GRAVE` or `MAST` (Move-Average Sampling Technique) requires pooling values across nodes $k$ that applied the **same action** $a(k) = (v_k, z_k) = a(j)$ anywhere inside a subtree or across the tree—which is specified in `Society Round 4` as **`V3.1` (Action-Identity & AMAF Masks)**:
  $$\mathcal{M}_{\text{act}}[j, k] \;=\; \mathbf{1}\big[\mathrm{step\_var}(j) = \mathrm{step\_var}(k) \land \mathrm{step\_val}(j) = \mathrm{step\_val}(k)\big],$$
  $$\mathcal{M}_{\text{amaf}}[j, k] \;=\; \mathbf{1}\Big[k \in \mathrm{Desc}(\mathrm{pa}(j)) \;\land\; \exists u \in \mathrm{Anc}(k) \cap \mathrm{Desc}(\mathrm{pa}(j)): a(u) = a(j)\Big].$$
- **How `GLC-AOM` Already Implements Continuous Latent-Space AMAF/GRAVE Sharing:** Even before adding discrete `V3.1` masks, `GLC-AOM` performs continuous cross-branch action-effect sharing through two mechanisms:
  1. **CDCL Global Literal-Overlap Broadcast (`src/search/neural_tree_controller.py:745–755`, `classical_teachers.py:905–916`):** Computes the exact literal-assignment inner product `lit_overlap = bmm(flat_lits, flat_lits.T)` across all nodes $j, k \in \{0, \dots, b-1\}$, which is an exact multi-literal generalization of $\mathcal{M}_{\text{act}}$ for conflict refutation!
  2. **2-Hop Tseitin Variable Co-Fluctuation Matrix (`Tseitin2HopQueryCoupler`, `neural_tree_controller.py:118–175`):** Pools parent-relative and ancestor-descendant variable polarities $C^{(1)}_{u,v} = \sum_{r=1}^{b-1} w_r d_r(u) d_r(v)$ across all branches of the tree and resolves 2-hop relays $C^{(2)}_{A,B}$ across intermediate variables $w$, writing the global cross-branch statistics back into `h_cons` at the root—acting as a continuous, second-order `GRAVE` refutation table over board variables!

### 3.2 `MCGS` (Saffidine, Cazenave, & Méhat 2012; Czech et al. 2020, 2021): Why Naive MCTS on a Transposition DAG Breaks and How `GLCSearch-v2` Handles Transpositions

#### 3.2.1 Saffidine, Cazenave, & Méhat (2012) `UCD` and Czech, Korus, & Kersting (2020, 2021) `MCGS`
In *"UCD: Upper Confidence Bound for Rooted Directed Acyclic Graphs"* (*Knowledge-Based Systems*, Vol. 34, pp. 26–33, 2012), **Saffidine, Cazenave, & Méhat** first formalized how state transpositions—where commuted move sequences $(a_1, b_1, a_2) \neq (a_2, b_1, a_1)$ lead to the identical canonical game state $\mathrm{canon}(s)$—turn a search tree $\mathcal{T}$ into a **Rooted Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)** $\mathcal{G} = (V, E)$. Subsequently, **Czech, Korus, & Kersting (2020, 2021)** (*"Monte-Carlo Graph Search for AlphaZero"*, arXiv:2012.11045 / *ECML-PKDD 2021*) and **Wu (2019–2021, `KataGo` MCGS)** scaled neural-guided Monte-Carlo Graph Search to AlphaZero architectures.

#### 3.2.2 Three Mathematical Pathologies That Break Naive MCTS on a Transposition DAG
When two distinct paths from the root $s_0$ merge into the same canonical state node $u \in V$, naive MCTS breaks down in three distinct ways (analyzed in detail by Saffidine et al. 2012, Czech et al. 2020 §3, and our `Society Round 4` audits [`02-glm53-round4-expressivity.md:70–78`](../../society/round4/02-glm53-round4-expressivity.md#L70-L78), [`03-gpt6-astra-round4-expressivity.md:360–420`](../../society/round4/03-gpt6-astra-round4-expressivity.md#L360-L420), and [`04-opus-round4-expressivity.md:221–244`](../../society/round4/04-opus-round4-expressivity.md#L221-L244)):

1. **Pathology 1 — Loss of Visit-Count Conservation ($N(u) \neq \sum_{a} N(u, a) + 1$ vs. $\sum_{p \in \mathrm{Pa}(u)} N(p, u)$):**
   In a tree $\mathcal{T}$, every non-root node $u$ has a unique parent edge $e = (\mathrm{pa}(u), u)$, so edge visits equal child node visits ($N(\mathrm{pa}(u), a) = N(u)$) and a node's visit count equals the cardinality of its descendant set ($N(u) = |\mathrm{Desc}(u)|$).
   In a transposition DAG $\mathcal{G}$, a node $u$ can have multiple incoming parent edges $(p_1, a_1) \to u$ and $(p_2, a_2) \to u$, and a descendant leaf $m \in \mathrm{Desc}_{\text{DAG}}(j)$ can be reachable from $j$ via $k(j, m) > 1$ distinct directed paths!
   - If one naively computes the matrix product $(E_{\text{trans}} \cdot \mathcal{M}_{\text{desc}})[j, m] = \sum_{\ell} E_{\text{trans}}[j, \ell]\mathcal{M}_{\text{desc}}[\ell, m]$, any descendant $m$ reachable through $k$ transposed intermediates is **double-counted $k$ times**, biasing the value mean by up to $2\big(1 - \frac{|\mathrm{Desc}_{\text{DAG}}(j)|}{\sum_m k(j, m)}\big)$ (`GLM-5.3` `E10`, [`02-glm53-round4-expressivity.md:74–76`](../../society/round4/02-glm53-round4-expressivity.md#L74-L76)).
   - Conversely, if PUCT uses child *node* visits $N(u)$ in the denominator $c_{\text{puct}} P(p_2, a_2) \frac{\sqrt{N(p_2)}}{1 + N(u)}$ when $u$ was already visited $1,000$ times via $p_1$ but $0$ times via $p_2$, $p_2$ under-explores action $a_2$; whereas if it uses only edge visits $N(p_2, a_2) = 0$, it ignores that $Q(u)$ is already tightly estimated to precision $1/\sqrt{1000}$!

2. **Pathology 2 — Stale Action-Value Backups Across Unvisited Ancestor Paths (The Off-Trajectory Backup Problem):**
   Suppose parent $p_1$ traverses edge $(p_1, a_1) \to u$ once when $Q(u) = +0.8$, recording $Q(p_1, a_1) = +0.8$. Later, parent $p_2$ traverses $(p_2, a_2) \to u$ for $50$ simulations and discovers a refutation in $u$'s subgraph, dropping $Q(u)$ from $+0.8$ to $-0.9$.
   Under standard trajectory-only backup (updating only ancestors along the current simulation path $s_0 \to \dots \to p_2 \to u$), **$p_1$'s edge value $Q(p_1, a_1)$ remains frozen at its stale value $+0.8$**!
   To maintain consistency across all incoming edges in a DAG, Czech et al. (2020) and `KataGo` define the **edge-visit-weighted MCGS node value fixed point**:
   $$Q^{\text{MCGS}}(u) \;=\; \frac{v_{\text{leaf}}(u) \;+\; \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(u)} N(u, a)\, Q^{\text{MCGS}}\big(\mathrm{child}(u, a)\big)}{1 \;+\; \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(u)} N(u, a)},$$
   and update edge values along the backup spine using the child's current consolidated $Q^{\text{MCGS}}(c)$ (or backpropagate value deltas $\Delta Q(u)$ weighted by edge visit shares).

3. **Pathology 3 — Graph-History Interaction (GHI) & Directed Cycles:**
   If canonical equivalence $E_{\text{trans}}[j, k]$ keys solely on board configuration without ply/repetition history or private-observation separation, reversible moves creates directed cycles ($j \in \mathrm{Desc}^{\text{DAG}}(k) \land k \in \mathrm{Desc}^{\text{DAG}}(j)$), and in imperfect-information games, merging states with identical public observations but distinct hidden states induces **strategy fusion** (`Opus 5.5` §1.4 Barrier 2, [`04-opus-round4-expressivity.md:229–243`](../../society/round4/04-opus-round4-expressivity.md#L229-L243)).

#### 3.2.3 How `GLCSearch-v2` Resolves Transposition DAGs (`V2.2` & Society-Hardened `V2.2'`)
`GLCSearch-v2` resolves all three DAG pathologies through a combination of canonical set hashing in `TreePagedKVBank` ([`src/search/kv_tree_search.py:406–410, 446–469`](../../src/search/kv_tree_search.py#L406-L410)) and the `V2.2` / `V2.2'` DAG operator in `GLCSearchTransformer` ([`src/search/neural_tree_controller.py:706–713`](../../src/search/neural_tree_controller.py#L706-L713), [`society/round4/ROUND4-SCOREBOARD.md:51`](../../society/round4/ROUND4-SCOREBOARD.md#L51)):
1. **Exact Canonical Assignment Equivalence $E_{\text{trans}}$:**
   In `TreePagedKVBank.get_relational_masks` (`kv_tree_search.py:406–407`), two tree nodes $j, k \in \{0, \dots, b-1\}$ are transposition-equivalent iff their clamped variable-value sets $O_j$ and $O_k$ are identical:
   $$E_{\text{trans}}[j, k] \;=\; \mathbf{1}\big[\mathrm{canon}(O_j) = \mathrm{canon}(O_k)\big] \;=\; \prod_{v=1}^V \mathbf{1}\big[(O_{j,v} = O_{k,v}) \land (Z_{j,v} \cdot O_{j,v} = Z_{k,v} \cdot O_{k,v})\big].$$
   Because each search step in `TreePagedKVBank` clamps a previously unclamped variable ($|O_{\mathrm{child}}| = |O_{\mathrm{parent}}| + 1$), the partial-order graph ordered by $|O_j|$ is **strictly acyclic by construction** (zero GHI cycles!).
2. **Boolean Reachability Closure $\mathcal{M}_{\text{desc}}^{\text{DAG}}$ (Zero Double-Counting):**
   Rather than summing integer path counts via raw matrix multiplication, `kv_tree_search.py:408–410` projects the product onto the **Boolean semiring** $(\{0, 1\}, \lor, \land)$:
   $$\mathcal{M}_{\text{desc}}^{\text{DAG}}[j, m] \;=\; \mathbf{1}\Big[ \big(\mathcal{M}_{\text{anc}} \cdot E_{\text{trans}} \cdot \mathcal{M}_{\text{anc}}\big)[j, m] > 0 \Big],$$
   and `Society Round 4`'s `V2.2'` (`02-glm53-round4-expressivity.md:71–76`, `04-opus-round4-expressivity.md:221–244`) generalizes this to the full **Boolean Kleene-star closure** via $\lceil \log_2 b \rceil$ rounds of Boolean matrix squaring:
   $$\mathcal{M}_{\text{desc}}^{\text{DAG}} \;=\; \big(E_{\text{tree}} \lor E_{\text{trans}}\big)^* \;=\; \big(I \lor E_{\text{tree}} \lor E_{\text{trans}}\big)^{\lceil \log_2 b \rceil}_{\text{Bool}},$$
   paired with **edge-visit-weighted MCGS spine backups** (`V2.1'`) and a strict firewall rule: *exact canonical equality $E_{\text{trans}}$ governs value/visit pooling (`Slots 15–16`), while soft hidden-state similarity $\exp(-\|h_j - h_k\|_2^2/\tau)$ is restricted strictly to the prior/proposal channel (`0` soft-merge value leaks in `R13-D4`)*.
   Furthermore, in `TreePagedKVBank.get_transposition_unique_mask` (`kv_tree_search.py:455–468`), duplicate canonical worlds are deduplicated before root normal-equation clearing so transposed cousin paths never double-count evidence at the root!

### 3.3 Proof-Number Search (`PNS`, Allis et al. 1994), `df-pn` (Nagai 2002), and Kishimoto et al. (2012)

#### 3.3.1 Allis, van der Meulen, & van den Herik (1994) Best-First Proof-Number Search (`PNS`)
In *"Proof-Number Search"* (*Artificial Intelligence*, Vol. 66(1), pp. 91–124, 1994), **L.V. Allis, M. van der Meulen, & H.J. van den Herik** introduced **Proof-Number Search (`PNS`)** for solving binary game-theoretic goals (win/loss or SAT/UNSAT) in AND/OR trees with nonuniform branching factors and variable proof depths (solving Connect-4 / Qubic / Go life-and-death).

At every node $j$ in an explicit AND/OR tree $\mathcal{T}$:
- The **proof number** $\mathrm{pn}(j) \in \mathbb{N} \cup \{0, \infty\}$ is the minimum number of leaf nodes in $\mathrm{Desc}(j)$ that must be **proven true** in order to prove $j = \text{TRUE}$ (an OR win).
- The **disproof number** $\mathrm{dn}(j) \in \mathbb{N} \cup \{0, \infty\}$ is the minimum number of leaf nodes in $\mathrm{Desc}(j)$ that must be **disproven** (proven false) in order to prove $j = \text{FALSE}$ (an AND win / OR loss).

**Exact Terminal, Frontier, and Interior Node Equations:**
1. **Solved Terminal Nodes:**
   - If $j$ is proven (`TRUE`): $\mathrm{pn}(j) = 0, \;\; \mathrm{dn}(j) = \infty$.
   - If $j$ is disproven (`FALSE`): $\mathrm{pn}(j) = \infty, \;\; \mathrm{dn}(j) = 0$.
2. **Unexpanded Frontier Leaf $j$ (Unit or Heuristic Initialization):**
   In standard unweighted PNS, $\mathrm{pn}(j) = \mathrm{dn}(j) = 1$; in heuristic PNS (`Allis et al. 1994 §3.3`), $\mathrm{pn}(j)$ and $\mathrm{dn}(j)$ are initialized to positive estimates $(h_{\mathrm{pn}}(j), h_{\mathrm{dn}}(j))$ of subtree proof/disproof complexity.
3. **Interior Node Backup over Children $\mathrm{Ch}(j)$ (Alternating $(\min, \sum)$ Semiring):**
   - **At an OR (MAX) Node $j$** (proven if *at least one* child is proven; disproven only if *all* children are disproven):
     $$\mathrm{pn}(j) \;=\; \min_{c \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)} \mathrm{pn}(c), \qquad \mathrm{dn}(j) \;=\; \sum_{c \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)} \mathrm{dn}(c).$$
   - **At an AND (MIN) Node $j$** (proven only if *all* children are proven; disproven if *at least one* child is disproven):
     $$\mathrm{pn}(j) \;=\; \sum_{c \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)} \mathrm{pn}(c), \qquad \mathrm{dn}(j) \;=\; \min_{c \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)} \mathrm{dn}(c).$$
4. **Selection of the Most-Proving Node (`MPN`):**
   Starting at root $0$, PNS descends top-down to an unexpanded leaf $j^*_{\text{MPN}}$ by choosing at every OR node the child $c$ with minimal $\mathrm{pn}(c)$, and at every AND node the child $c$ with minimal $\mathrm{dn}(c)$:
   $$c^*(j) \;=\; \begin{cases} \arg\min_{c \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)} \mathrm{pn}(c) & \text{if } j \text{ is an OR node}, \\[4pt] \arg\min_{c \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)} \mathrm{dn}(c) & \text{if } j \text{ is an AND node}. \end{cases}$$
   Expanding $j^*_{\text{MPN}}$ is guaranteed to immediately alter either $\mathrm{pn}(0)$ or $\mathrm{dn}(0)$ along the ancestor spine $\mathrm{Anc}(j^*_{\text{MPN}})$.

#### 3.3.2 Nagai (2002) Depth-First Proof-Number Search (`df-pn`) & Kishimoto et al. (2012)
Because best-first `PNS` stores the entire search tree in RAM, it exhausts memory on deep endgame solvers. In *"Df-pn Algorithm for Searching AND/OR Trees and Its Applications"* (Ph.D. Thesis, Department of Information Science, University of Tokyo, 2002), **Ayumu Nagai** proved that best-first `PNS` can be converted into an equivalent **depth-first iterative-threshold algorithm (`df-pn`)** using a transposition table and two threshold parameters $\big(\mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{pn}}(j), \, \mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{dn}}(j)\big)$ passed top-down from parent $j$ to its most-proving child $c_1$:

Let $c_1 \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)$ be the child with the smallest $\mathrm{pn}$ (at an OR node) or smallest $\mathrm{dn}$ (at an AND node), and let $c_2 \in \mathrm{Ch}(j) \setminus \{c_1\}$ be the second-best child (with $\mathrm{pn}(c_2) = \infty$ or $\mathrm{dn}(c_2) = \infty$ if $|\mathrm{Ch}(j)| = 1$). `df-pn` continues searching inside $c_1$'s subtree so long as $\mathrm{pn}(c_1) < \mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{pn}}(c_1)$ and $\mathrm{dn}(c_1) < \mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{dn}}(c_1)$, where:
- **At an OR Node $j$:**
  $$\mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{pn}}(c_1) \;=\; \min\!\big(\mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{pn}}(j), \;\; \mathrm{pn}(c_2) + 1\big), \qquad \mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{dn}}(c_1) \;=\; \mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{dn}}(j) \;-\; \mathrm{dn}(j) \;+\; \mathrm{dn}(c_1).$$
- **At an AND Node $j$:**
  $$\mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{pn}}(c_1) \;=\; \mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{pn}}(j) \;-\; \mathrm{pn}(j) \;+\; \mathrm{pn}(c_1), \qquad \mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{dn}}(c_1) \;=\; \min\!\big(\mathrm{th}_{\mathrm{dn}}(j), \;\; \mathrm{dn}(c_2) + 1\big).$$

**Nagai's Equivalence Theorem (2002):** On a tree, `df-pn` always expands the **exact same Most-Proving Node** as best-first `PNS`, while expanding fewer interior pointers by staying locally around the current MPN until the sibling threshold $\mathrm{pn}(c_2) + 1$ (or ancestor budget) is exceeded!
**Kishimoto, Winands, Müller, & Saito (2012)** (*"Game-Tree Search Using Proof Numbers: The First Twenty Years"*, *ICGA Journal*, Vol. 35(3), pp. 131–156) surveyed two decades of PNS/df-pn enhancements, including `PN^2` (two-level PNS), `df-pn(r)` for DAGs and GHI repetition handling in Tsume-Shogi/Go/Hex/Checkers, and **MCTS-Solver / PN-MCTS** hybrids (Winands, Björnsson, & Saito, 2008) that integrate $(\mathrm{pn}, \mathrm{dn})$ solver flags into MCTS nodes.

#### 3.3.3 How `GLCSearch-v2` Resolves the $\mathsf{NC}^1$ Alternating Semiring Barrier (`Theorem R4.3` Barrier 1 / `V2.1'`)
Why could `GLCSearch-v1` represent arbitrary-depth MCTS in $L=3$ layers (`Theorem R4.2`) yet fail on deep `Minimax` and `PNS` (`Theorem R4.3` Barrier 1)?
- **Circuit Complexity Separation ($\mathsf{TC}^0$ vs. $\mathsf{NC}^1$, [`society/round4/04-opus-round4-expressivity.md:193–202`](../../society/round4/04-opus-round4-expressivity.md#L193-L202)):**
  MCTS backup $Q^{\text{MCTS}}(j) = \frac{1}{|\mathrm{Desc}(j)|} \sum_{k \in \mathrm{Desc}(j)} s_j s_k v_k$ is a **commutative monoid** over $\mathrm{Desc}(j)$, computable in 1 threshold/attention layer ($\mathsf{TC}^0$) once the transitive mask $\mathcal{M}_{\text{desc}}$ is supplied.
  By contrast, both Minimax ($\max_{c_1} \min_{c_2} \max_{c_3} \dots$) and PNS ($\text{OR}: (\min, \sum); \;\text{AND}: (\sum, \min)$) alternate two **non-commuting, non-distributive operators** at every ply. Evaluating a depth-$D$ alternating Boolean/semiring tree is **$\mathsf{NC}^1$-complete** (Buss, 1987), whereas any fixed-$L$-layer log-precision Transformer lies in uniform $\mathsf{TC}^0$ (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023). Thus, unless $\mathsf{TC}^0 = \mathsf{NC}^1$, a non-recurrent $L$-layer Transformer (`GLCSearch-v1`, $L=3$) suffers an irreducible depth barrier whenever tree depth $D > L$!
- **Exact Resolution in `GLCSearch-v2` (`V2.1` & `V2.1'`, [`src/search/neural_tree_controller.py:655–705`](../../src/search/neural_tree_controller.py#L655-L705), [`src/search/classical_teachers.py:686–818`](../../src/search/classical_teachers.py#L686-L818)):**
  `GLCSearch-v2` initializes heuristic leaf proof/disproof numbers from leaf win probabilities $p_{\text{win}}(j) = \sigma(2 v_j) \in [0.05, 0.95]$ (`pn = 2(1 - p_win)`, `dn = 2 p_win`, lines `660–662`) and executes an exact bottom-up ply/spine recurrence from $d = d_{\max}-1$ down to $0$ (`lines 665–678`):
  $$\mathrm{pn}(j) \leftarrow \begin{cases} \min_{c \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)} \mathrm{pn}(c) & \text{if } d(j) \equiv 0 \pmod 2, \\ \sum_{c \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)} \mathrm{pn}(c) & \text{if } d(j) \equiv 1 \pmod 2, \end{cases} \qquad \mathrm{dn}(j) \leftarrow \begin{cases} \sum_{c \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)} \mathrm{dn}(c) & \text{if } d(j) \equiv 0 \pmod 2, \\ \min_{c \in \mathrm{Ch}(j)} \mathrm{dn}(c) & \text{if } d(j) \equiv 1 \pmod 2, \end{cases}$$
  alongside top-down $[\alpha(j), \beta(j)]$ window propagation (`lines 680–697`), feeding the resulting $(\mathrm{pn}_j, \mathrm{dn}_j, V^{\text{MM}}_j, \alpha_j, \beta_j)$ into slots `3`, `17`, and `18` of $\psi_j$ (`lines 698–705, 757, 763–764`). In `R13-D2` ([`benchmarks/run_r13_algo_imitation.py:2662–2668`](../../benchmarks/run_r13_algo_imitation.py#L2662-L2668)), this lifts OOD depth-$12$ value $R^2$ by $\ge +0.18$ over `GLCSearch-v1`!

### 3.4 `Gumbel MuZero` (Danihelka et al., 2022) & Sequential Halving (Karnin et al., 2013): Why PUCT Fails at Small Budgets $B \in \{2..16\}$ and How `GLCSearch-v2`'s Sibling Rank Percentile Head (`V2.4'`) Implements Sequential Halving in 1 Layer

#### 3.4.1 Why AlphaZero's PUCT Fails to Guarantee Policy Improvement at Small Budgets $B \in \{2, \dots, 16\}$
In AlphaZero (Silver et al., 2017, 2018) and MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020), the root node selects actions according to the deterministic PUCT rule:
$$a_t \;=\; \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \left\{\, \hat{Q}(a) \;+\; c_{\text{puct}} \, \pi_{\text{prior}}(a) \frac{\sqrt{\sum_b N(b)}}{1 + N(a)} \,\right\},$$
and after $B$ simulations acts or trains on the normalized visit-count distribution $\hat{\pi}_{\text{visit}}(a) = \frac{N(a)^{1/\tau}}{\sum_b N(b)^{1/\tau}}$.

In *"Policy Improvement by Planning with Gumbel"* (*ICLR 2022*, **Ivo Danihelka, Arthur Guez, Julian Schrittwieser, & David Silver**), the authors proved a striking failure mode of PUCT when the simulation budget $B \in \{2, 4, 8, 16\}$ is smaller than or comparable to the number of legal actions $|\mathcal{A}|$:
1. **Unvisited Actions Receive Zero Visit Probability ($\hat{\pi}_{\text{visit}}(a) = 0$):**
   If $B < |\mathcal{A}|$ (for example, $B = 8$ simulations on a board with $|\mathcal{A}| = 36$ variables/moves), at least $|\mathcal{A}| - B = 28$ actions have $N(a) = 0$. Even if an unvisited action $a'$ has a higher true value $q_\pi(s, a')$ than a visited action $a$ that suffered an unlucky rollout, $\hat{\pi}_{\text{visit}}(a') = 0$, so **acting or training on visit counts $N(a)/B$ can strictly degrade the policy**:
   $$\sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \hat{\pi}_{\text{visit}}(a) \, q_\pi(s, a) \;<\; \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi_{\text{prior}}(a) \, q_\pi(s, a).$$
2. **Cumulative-Regret vs. Simple-Regret Allocation:**
   PUCT/UCB is designed to minimize *cumulative regret* (repeatedly pulling the best arm to collect reward during exploration), whereas test-time tree search at the root is a **pure exploration / fixed-budget best-arm identification problem** (minimizing *simple regret* of the final chosen action after $B$ pulls).
3. **Lack of Stochastic Exploration Without Ad-Hoc Dirichlet Noise:**
   Because PUCT is deterministic given $\pi_{\text{prior}}$ and $\hat{Q}$, AlphaZero requires injecting ad-hoc root Dirichlet noise $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)$, which corrupts the prior logits without guaranteeing policy improvement.

#### 3.4.2 Danihelka et al. (2022) `Gumbel MuZero` & Karnin, Koren, & Somekh (2013) `Sequential Halving`
Danihelka et al. (2022) solved all three flaws by combining the **Gumbel-Top-$k$ trick** (sampling $m$ candidate actions without replacement from $\pi_{\text{prior}}$) with **Sequential Halving** (**Karnin, Koren, & Somekh, 2013**, *"Almost Optimal Exploration in Multi-Armed Bandits"*, *ICML 2013*, PMLR Vol. 28(3), pp. 1238–1246):

1. **Gumbel Sampling Without Replacement at the Root:**
   Draw i.i.d. standard Gumbel variables $g(a) \sim \mathrm{Gumbel}(0, 1)$ ($g(a) = -\log(-\log U_a), \, U_a \sim \mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$) for all $a \in \mathcal{A}$, and initialize the active candidate set $S_1 \subseteq \mathcal{A}$ as the top-$m$ actions ($m = \min(B, |\mathcal{A}|)$) maximizing $g(a) + \mathrm{logits}(a)$, where $\mathrm{logits}(a) = \log \pi_{\text{prior}}(a)$.
2. **Sequential Halving Across $P = \lceil \log_2 m \rceil$ Phases (Karnin et al., 2013):**
   Divide the total simulation budget $B$ equally across $P = \lceil \log_2 m \rceil$ phases. In phase $p \in \{1, \dots, P\}$ with $|S_p| = \lceil m / 2^{p-1} \rceil$ surviving actions:
   - Allocate to **each** surviving action $a \in S_p$ an equal number of additional simulations:
     $$n_p \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{B}{P \, |S_p|} \right\rfloor \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{B}{\lceil \log_2 m \rceil \, \lceil m / 2^{p-1} \rceil} \right\rfloor.$$
   - Update the empirical action value $\hat{q}(a)$ for all $a \in S_p$ and compute the **Gumbel + Prior + Completed-$Q$ Score**:
     $$\mathrm{Score}_{\text{Gumbel}}(a) \;=\; g(a) \;+\; \mathrm{logits}(a) \;+\; \sigma\big(\hat{q}(a)\big),$$
     where $\sigma(\hat{q}(a))$ is the monotonically increasing visit-scaled transformation:
     $$\sigma\big(\hat{q}(a)\big) \;=\; \Big(c_{\text{visit}} + \max_{b \in \mathcal{A}} N(b)\Big)\, c_{\text{scale}} \, \hat{q}(a) \qquad (c_{\text{visit}} = 50.0, \;\; c_{\text{scale}} = 0.1).$$
   - **Ordinal Rank Elimination Rule:** Sort $S_p$ by $\mathrm{Score}_{\text{Gumbel}}(a)$ and retain only the **top half by ordinal rank** for phase $p+1$:
     $$S_{p+1} \;=\; \left\{\, a \in S_p \;:\; \mathrm{rank}_{S_p}(a) \;\le\; \left\lceil \frac{|S_p|}{2} \right\rceil \,\right\}, \qquad \text{where } \mathrm{rank}_{S_p}(a) \;=\; 1 + \sum_{s \in S_p \setminus \{a\}} \mathbf{1}\big[\mathrm{Score}_{\text{Gumbel}}(s) > \mathrm{Score}_{\text{Gumbel}}(a)\big].$$
   After phase $P$, a single action $A_{\text{SH}} \in S_{P+1}$ ($|S_{P+1}| = 1$) remains!
3. **Completed $Q$-Values & Improved Policy Target $\pi'(\cdot)$:**
   For policy network training (and deterministic non-root selection $\arg\max_a \big(\pi'(a) - \frac{N(a)}{1 + \sum_b N(b)}\big)$), Gumbel MuZero defines **completed $Q$-values** $\mathrm{completedQ}(a)$ that fill in unvisited actions ($N(a)=0$) using the mixed value estimator $v_{\text{mix}}$:
   $$\mathrm{completedQ}(a) \;=\; \begin{cases} \hat{q}(a) & \text{if } N(a) > 0, \\[4pt] v_{\text{mix}}(s) & \text{if } N(a) = 0, \end{cases} \qquad v_{\text{mix}}(s) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \sum_b N(b)}\left(\hat{v}(s) + \frac{\sum_b N(b)}{\sum_{b: N(b)>0} \pi(b)} \sum_{b: N(b)>0} \pi(b)\,\hat{q}(b)\right),$$
   and trains the policy network via cross-entropy against the analytically improved distribution $\pi'(a) = \mathrm{softmax}\big(\mathrm{logits}(a) + \sigma(\mathrm{completedQ}(a))\big)$.
   **Policy Improvement Theorem (Danihelka et al., 2022, Proposition 1):** For any monotonic $\sigma(\cdot)$ and exact values $q_\pi$, both $\mathbb{E}_g[q_\pi(s, \arg\max_a(g(a) + \mathrm{logits}(a) + \sigma(q_\pi(s, a))))] \ge \sum_a \pi(a) q_\pi(s, a)$ and $\sum_a \pi'(a) q_\pi(s, a) \ge \sum_a \pi(a) q_\pi(s, a)$ hold with strict inequality whenever $q_\pi$ is non-constant on $\mathrm{supp}(\pi)$—even for budget $B = 2$!

#### 3.4.3 Why `GLCSearch-v1` Fails on Sequential Halving (`Theorem R4.3` Barrier 4) and How `GLCSearch-v2`'s Sibling Rank Percentile Head (`V2.4'`) Implements It in 1 Layer
In `Society Round 4` ([`society/round4/01-expressivity-theorems-and-mcts-gap.md:144–150`](../../society/round4/01-expressivity-theorems-and-mcts-gap.md#L144-L150), [`02-glm53-round4-expressivity.md:89`](../../society/round4/02-glm53-round4-expressivity.md#L89), [`04-opus-round4-expressivity.md:284–306`](../../society/round4/04-opus-round4-expressivity.md#L284-L306)):
- **Why Softmax Sibling Attention (`v1`) Cannot Compute Sequential Halving:**
  Eliminating the bottom half of sibling candidates requires testing the **ordinal median/quantile condition** $\mathrm{rank}_{\text{sib}}(j) < \lceil m_{\text{sib}} / 2^p \rceil$. A standard softmax or mean attention head over $\mathcal{M}_{\text{sib}}$ computes a convex combination weighted by $e^{\beta S(s)}$; because $e^{\beta S(s)}$ depends on the *cardinal magnitudes* and skewness of the scores $\{S(s)\}_{s \in \mathrm{Sib}(j)}$ (e.g., one outlier sibling with a huge score vs. five closely clustered siblings), no fixed linear readout of softmax-pooled sibling values can determine whether node $j$ ranks in the top $50\%$ of its sibling group!
- **Exact 1-Layer Resolution in `GLCSearch-v2` (`V2.4` / `V2.4'`, [`src/search/neural_tree_controller.py:734–743`](../../src/search/neural_tree_controller.py#L734-L743), [`src/search/classical_teachers.py:1179–1283`](../../src/search/classical_teachers.py#L1179-L1283)):**
  1. First, `GLCSearch-v2` computes the maximum sibling visit count $\max_{s \in \mathrm{Sib}(j) \cup \{j\}} N(s)$ via a masked max/LSE reduction over $\mathcal{M}_{\text{sib}} \lor \mathcal{M}_{\text{self}}$ (`neural_tree_controller.py:735`), forms the Gumbel MuZero completed-$Q$ transform $\sigma(\hat{Q}(j)) = (50.0 + \max_{s} N(s)) \cdot 0.1 \cdot Q^{\text{MCTS}}(j)$ (`line 736`), and evaluates $G(j) = \log p(j \mid \mathrm{pa}(j)) + \sigma(\hat{Q}(j)) + g_j$ (`line 737`).
  2. Next, `V2.4'` applies a **Pairwise Sibling Dominance / Rank-Percentile Operator** across the $b \times b$ sibling mask $\mathcal{M}_{\text{sib}}[j, s] = \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{pa}(j) = \mathrm{pa}(s) \land j \neq s]$ (`lines 738–741`):
     $$\widehat{\mathrm{rank}}_{\text{sib}}(j) \;=\; \sum_{s=0}^{b-1} \mathcal{M}_{\text{sib}}[j, s] \cdot \sigma\!\left(\frac{G(s) - G(j)}{\tau_{\text{rank}}}\right), \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{pct}}_{\text{sib}}(j) \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\widehat{\mathrm{rank}}_{\text{sib}}(j)}{|\mathrm{Sib}(j) \cup \{j\}|}.$$
  3. **Certified Temperature Bound (`Opus 5.5` §1.4 & `GLM-5.3` `E12`):** Let $\Delta = \min_{j \neq s \in \mathrm{Sib}(j)} |G(j) - G(s)|$ be the minimum pairwise score gap among $m = |\mathrm{Sib}(j) \cup \{j\}|$ siblings. Since $\big|\sigma(x/\tau_{\text{rank}}) - \mathbf{1}[x > 0]\big| \le e^{-|x|/\tau_{\text{rank}}}$, the rank-percentile approximation error satisfies:
     $$\big|\widehat{\mathrm{pct}}_{\text{sib}}(j) - \mathrm{pct}_{\text{sib}}(j)\big| \;\le\; e^{-\Delta / \tau_{\text{rank}}}.$$
     Because the Sequential Halving survivor threshold sits at half-integer multiples of $1/m$, choosing $\tau_{\text{rank}} < \frac{\Delta}{\log(2m)}$ (with index tie-breaking via $\mathcal{M}_{\text{sib}<}$ when $\Delta = 0$) guarantees $|\widehat{\mathrm{pct}}_{\text{sib}}(j) - \mathrm{pct}_{\text{sib}}(j)| < \frac{1}{2m}$, so thresholding $\mathrm{survived\_sh}(j) = \mathbf{1}\big[\mathrm{rank}_{\text{sib}}(j) < \lceil \frac{1}{2} |\mathrm{Sib}(j) \cup \{j\}| \rceil\big]$ (`line 741`) recovers the **exact Sequential Halving survivor set in 1 layer**!
  4. Finally, `GLCSearch-v2` writes both $Q^{\text{Gumbel-SH}}(j) = \tanh(\frac{1}{2} G(j))$ and the halving selection score $s_{\text{SH}}(j) = 2.5\,\mathrm{survived\_sh}(j) + 0.8\,Q^{\text{Gumbel-SH}}(j) - 0.6\,\frac{\mathrm{rank}_{\text{sib}}(j)}{m} + \frac{1}{N(j) + 0.5}$ into operator basis slots `22` and `23` (`lines 742–743, 768–769`), allocating the next simulation visit to the least-visited surviving top-half sibling—matching Karnin et al. (2013) and Danihelka et al. (2022) in $O(1)$ depth!
