# Active structure elicitation from a noisy, biased forecaster: literature note (2026-09-24)

Goal: recover the dependency structure of Qwen3-32B's beliefs over n binary events using marginal, joint, conditional and screening-list queries, chosen by expected information gain (EIG). The oracle is noisy (sampling, rewording) and biased (AND too high, OR too low).

## Bayesian optimal experimental design
Lindley (1956) defines the value of an experiment as the expected reduction in Shannon entropy of the posterior, which is the EIG criterion ([doi:10.1214/aoms/1177728069](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177728069)). Chaloner and Verdinelli (1995) review the decision-theoretic framing ([doi:10.1214/ss/1177009939](https://doi.org/10.1214/ss/1177009939)). Rainforth et al. (2023) cover modern EIG estimators, amortized and sequential designs ([arXiv:2302.14545](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14545)). BatchBALD (Kirsch et al. 2019) scores a batch by joint mutual information rather than a sum of per-query scores, which avoids redundant batches ([arXiv:1906.08158](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08158)).

## Active learning of Bayes-net and causal structure
Tong and Koller (2001) choose experiments that reduce the posterior loss over Bayes-net structures ([IJCAI PDF](https://ai.stanford.edu/~koller/Papers/Tong+Koller:IJCAI01.pdf)). Murphy (2001, UC Berkeley tech report) does the same with an MCMC posterior over DAGs ([Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Active-Learning-of-Causal-Bayes-Net-Structure-Murphy/13c2ad5c1dc96f651b2bc197089f62b0691fa622)). Later work scales this up. ABCD (Agrawal et al., AISTATS 2019) runs budgeted, targeted batch design with a submodular EIG objective ([arXiv:1902.10347](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10347)). Tigas et al. (NeurIPS 2022) pick both the intervention target and its value ([arXiv:2203.02016](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02016)). Toth et al. (NeurIPS 2022) aim EIG at a causal query of interest rather than at the full graph ([arXiv:2206.02063](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02063)).

## Expert elicitation of probabilities and belief networks
O'Hagan et al. (2006), *Uncertain Judgements*, is the standard reference on eliciting probabilities, covering heuristics, biases and calibration ([doi:10.1002/0470033312](https://doi.org/10.1002/0470033312)). Renooij (2001) surveys practical elicitation for belief networks, including response modes and bias ([doi:10.1017/S0269888901000145](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0269888901000145)). Druzdzel and van der Gaag's TKDE special-issue introduction (2000) frames the "where do the numbers come from" problem ([doi:10.1109/tkde.2000.868901](https://doi.org/10.1109/tkde.2000.868901)).

## Coherentization and bias models
Osherson and Vardi (2006) aggregate incoherent estimates by projecting them onto the nearest coherent probability assignment ([doi:10.1016/j.geb.2006.04.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geb.2006.04.001)). Predd et al. (2008) extend this to experts who abstain on some events, with scalable algorithms ([doi:10.1287/deca.1080.0119](https://doi.org/10.1287/deca.1080.0119)). Karvetski et al. (2013) weight each judge by how coherent their answers are, which improves accuracy ([doi:10.1287/deca.2013.0279](https://doi.org/10.1287/deca.2013.0279)). Costello and Watts (2014) show that "probability theory plus noise" (random read errors in a frequency estimate) reproduces the conjunction and disjunction fallacies ([doi:10.1037/a0037010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037010)).

## LLMs as causal-graph oracles
Kiciman et al. (2023) find that LLMs answer pairwise causal-direction questions well but make unpredictable errors ([arXiv:2305.00050](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.00050)). Pairwise querying needs O(n^2) calls. Jiralerspong et al. (2024) replace it with a breadth-first expansion that asks "what does X cause?", using O(n) calls ([arXiv:2402.01207](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01207)). Vashishtha et al. (2023) ask the LLM only for a causal order, which is more robust to an imperfect expert than full edge sets ([arXiv:2310.15117](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.15117)).

## What applies when the oracle is noisy and biased
- **Model the answer process in the likelihood.** EIG is only as good as p(answer | structure). Without explicit noise and bias terms it treats rewording variance as signal and keeps re-querying noisy pairs. Put a per-query-type noise scale and bias parameters in the model and infer them jointly with the structure.
- **Use Costello-Watts as the bias form.** Under their model the expected estimate is (1-2d)P + d, a regression toward 0.5. The read-error rate d is larger for more complex events, so AND answers come out too high and OR answers too low. This gives a small parametric bias model, fitted per query type, instead of free offsets.
- **Coherentize, then weight.** Before reading off dependencies, project each answer set onto the coherent polytope (Osherson-Vardi, Predd). Then downweight prompts or rewordings whose answers are less coherent (Karvetski). Structure inferred from raw incoherent joints will report spurious dependencies.
- **Make batches diverse.** Greedy top-k EIG selects near-duplicate pairs that share an event. Use joint-MI scoring (BatchBALD) or submodular greedy selection (ABCD) instead.
- **Screen first, then query pairs.** Screening lists in the style of Jiralerspong's BFS prune candidate parents, so the O(n^2) joint and conditional queries only cover the flagged pairs. Treat screening answers as noisy, low-cost evidence with their own false-negative rate, not as hard pruning.
- **Conditionals are not interventions.** P(B|A) is observational conditioning inside the model's beliefs, not do(A), so it identifies dependence and Markov equivalence classes, not direction. Rewordings and repeated samples act like repeated measurements: they reduce noise cheaply (roughly as 1/sqrt(k)) but leave shared bias untouched, which only the bias model or coherentization corrects.
