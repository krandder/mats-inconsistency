LLM-agents, such as:

(a) An agent performing “reasoning”, including making “internal simulations”, in the process of making a decision/action
(b) An agent performing “deep research”, by searching for data/evidence/sources, in the process of making a prediction/report

Are both learned optimizers, doing “mesa-optimization”. In these cases, as in most cases for learned optimizers, the exact “target” of this inner optimization is unknown. The preferences, or the internal states the system “steers” towards, and the “beliefs”, as they change over time, are often opaque. This process of “training the optimizer” (e.g. on RL environments) can alter the beliefs the agent may have, as well as reinforce internal drives that may now be known, and that we may not know how they’ll generalize.

Examples: it is possible that, because of its other goals, it will not perform real-world harm. So when trained on apparently-harmful situations, the model may be reinforced to believe it is in a simulation. Re/ “drives”; the model can develop the drive to “understanding/manipulating the Grader” who is responsible for its evaluation, or any other set of drives.

Here is a new ambitious agenda to try to seek “Robust Alignment” in the “Risks from Learned Optimization” sense.

The agenda is to seek an “explicit mesa-optimizer”.

        Instead of trying to ensure we can “understand” this inner learned optimizer, we’ll try to contain and show that this optimizer has a specific “target” T that we understand in the limits of compute. This target T is “external” to this inner optimizer, but defined in relation to some “subspace” of a world model M that is assumed to be understood. This world model may be defined in terms of a pretrained model, with the subset restricting that space further. The motivation for this is as follows. For extremely advanced AIs, it may be very difficult to “fully understand” any advanced optimizer. For instance, an AI that is far-superhuman at math is expected to have internal concepts related to math that are beyond the current understanding of any humans. Similarly, an AI that is superhuman at go will have “internal concepts” related to go that no human possesses. However, just like in superhuman Go play, we can be confident that these “alien Go concepts” are still being used by the optimizer (the MCTS) for the well-understood purpose of (winning the game assuming itself as a player), we can seek and hope to have more general superhuman optimizers that also optimize for “well-understood” concepts, even as it uses “alien” concepts to search how to do that.


For Go this is relatively simple, because the world-model is exact and can be simulated cheaply, and we know an algorithm (MCTS) that is guaranteed to converge to the right answer with enough computation. We’d like to be able to extend this to the case where the world-model we’re optimizing *on* is uncertain. So that, just like in MCTS with UCT, network errors only cost efficiency. If we can achieve that, we can hope to isolate the “alien concepts” inside something akin to a “heuristics network” or “search policy”, that is trained on some kind of self-play to improve the efficiency of some search procedure. Even if in practice, with limited compute, any prediction or action made by an agent created this way will still depend on the concepts represented on this “search policy”, the fact that in the limit the actions/beliefs should not depend on this search policy can give us some theoretical grounding that “alien biases” in what the internal optimizer is looking for are expected to be self-correcting with more compute.

How can we even begin to approach this agenda? The first thing is to try to figure out what the right optimization target T is supposed to be, when we have an uncertain world-model. How to even think about “search” in such a model?

One way to reason about this is to understand that an “uncertain world-model”, such as what we may have internally represented in a transformer after “pretraining”, is also an “inconsistent” world model. What do we mean by that? We can think about this by understanding forward vs any-order transformers. Normal transformer LLMs are trained on next-token prediction. But instead you could have, like BERT, a transformer that is bidirectional and that can predict any token, with some subset of tokens known on either “side”. For such “any-order” transformers, the probability of a sample “completion” is different if you aggregate in different “query-orders”. Some orders (like predicting the next-token, regular order) are noticeably easier than others (like predicting the tokens in a backward order) [show paper or experiments]. But a real encoded “joint distribution” of a set of tokens would be independent of order. For normal finetuned-LLMs, or LLMs finetuned for prediction tasks (including conditional predictions), if we make predictions x_1, then x_2|x_1, …, x_n|x_{<n}, or if we do it in a different query-order (by asking the questions in different order in the same ‘linear’ context), we also get the same kind of inconsistency. So transformers are “naturally inconsistent” in a deep way, including when trained only on “forward-token prediction”, as soon as you can arrange different “orders” in a semantic space. Initially I was interested in the idea of fine tuning LLMs to become more consistent in their predictions for such scenarios. But quite curiously, doing so seems to hurt prediction accuracy [more evidence later]. It is not a matter of LLMs becoming more “confident-and-wrong”; forcing consistency between random orders often “averages out” what I think are “stiff” (predictions the model is confident about) and “non-stiff” basically guesses. This is different from just the entropy of the predictions; a model could in theory be very confident that some distribution is really 0.5-0.5 between two alternatives A and B, and this is different from “guessing” whether its A or B due to not being able to figure out in time. E.g. for a transformer predicting tokens, if the “reverse” order is harder, then more of the predictions will be “guesses” trying to make the two “forward” and “reverse” predictions more consistent with each other then just degrades the quality of the “forward” predictions, turning the confident one more into “guessing”. (Note: there maybe is a better way to train such an any-order network, or a normal LLM on any-order prediction tasks, to make them better. This whole discussion of stiff and not-stiff made me thing that we can possibly just use the perplexity over different “orders” as some kind of weight; for estimating the probability of some state of the joint, the “more-stiff” predictions naturally correspond to the “orders” that should have more weight in a way (not sure how much…).

So here is one way to think about this uncertain and inconsistent world-model. We can think about it using the theory of Probabilistic Dependency Graphs (PDGs). For each possible “order/permutation”, we have a different prediction of the same thing. If for each per-step prediction we can have a /beta for the “trust” in the PDG sense that we give to this edge, then we can try to minimize the inconsistency over the whole set of permutations. We can maybe also use a “natural order policy prior” to determine the trust maybe; so the normal trust of each trajectory is “weighted” according to this “order prior” (I need to understand better what this order prior is, or what other kind of trust \beta we want to use as a conjecture/idea for this…). But it seems to me that it should be possible to define some kind of inconsistency-based measure! For normal text, it may not seem like there would be a “much better way/order” for predicting things, so we don’t imagine this would help much. But maybe e.g. for other scenarios, where e.g. we have “pretraining over sudoku (puzzles, solutions)” pairs, in structured format, there are some right-order for predicting tokens that would make it much easier. And if we’re allowed to “extend” this to more abstract spaces, so that we can define more “dimensions” or “concepts” to extend/predict, then it may be possible to find “augmented orders” that make a lot more sense or make the problem much easier to solve… and maybe we can find such “spaces” that we can understand, and define targets over, inside pretrained network,. Anyway… we were supposed to talk about the target for now. So what can we say about the target? It seems like we can define these targets precisely, for a whole class, and we can maybe know what the target is at the limit, e.g. by defining it as some expectation over a full permutation of orders. 

What kinds of targets can we possibly encode by some procedure like this? A wide range of things! For instance, we can try to use this similarly to the “Scientist AI” agenda of Bengio/LawZero (we need to determine in which ways this is the same agenda, or a separate agenda), to find a better model that can “do reasoning and seek the truth” (consistent with a set of initial truth-labels). But we can also think of (something, cite “Agents as Web of Beliefs”) of beliefs-and-preferences as being the same kind of thing. So in this sense “actions” are just beliefs that are ~100% self-fulfilling; this helps us frame “abstract actions” in the same way as “abstract beliefs” without needing to distinguish this with complex “hard-coded” hierarchical models. Once we think about this in this way, we can represent an utility function as a prior over some beliefs (about the future), and these once encoded are represented “in the same way” that other priors work (see Richardson’s LIR with the “faith edge”). This gives us hope that any method we can use to “search for the best beliefs consistent with these world-model priors” can also be used in almost the same way to “search for the best plans/actions consistent with my priors and my goals/utility”. But then we have to ensure we’ll not have “wishful thinking”


In some way, this proposal is inspired by Christiano’s “Iterated Distillation and Amplification”. The “amplification” is what we’re discussing with a search procedure, and the most important part. But we need to be able to perform “distillation” in order to achieve greater capabilities in achieving this objective (just like AlphaZero has to perform distillation after the MCTS amplification step); this is the way to make the “search heuristic” better over time. This will inevitably lead to “alien concepts” being learned inside this policy; so the search policy can be defined by a network / set of parameters that is much larger than the set of parameters that define the base-world-model W and the target T. Crucial hypothesis for this alignment agenda: that the space W in which we define our goals/utility can be represented with much fewer parameters than the “policy/heuristic” network that encodes for all the alien concepts which determine how the agent plans to effectively maximize the utility or reason about the world.

There are many things that are not clear on this agenda. Let me enumerate them.


(1) How this relates to “knightian uncertainty” and “trust boundaries” between agents. One way I have tried to think about this is by thinking more formally about active inference POMDP models (link to Richard’s article). Initially the agent does not represent the right concepts “s” in a world-model, the agent is in a “first-person” perspective absorbing a stream of observations. The “hidden variables representing state” – “s” – get learned, in these models, form the two matrices: the matrix “A” that encodes observations p(O|s,A); and the matrix B that encodes transitions p(s_{n+1}|s_{n}, B). This space “s” has to in some sense remain fixed as we want to define utilities over “s” here in this agenda. Any time A or B changes, this changes the meaning of the subspace and leads to “ontological crises”. Either way, once we have “learned” the “right” matrixes A and B, such that the p(O|s,A,B) explains “everything” that we have (or that we care about) about the observations, then we’re close to the “third-person” mode where we can reduce “variational free energy” to best estimate “s”, at least so far as this is “all we need to know”. I am still confused about this connection of the agenda, but it seems to me that this POMDP and active-inference formalism can help us reason more precisely about many of these problems.
