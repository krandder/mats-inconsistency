Noisy-channel interpretability

Kelvin Azevedo Santos, MATS autumn 2026 (mentor: Deger Turan). 28 October 2026. Research proposal, no results yet. Thanks to Samuel Liew for the conversation that started it.

1. Proposal

We want to approach mechanistic interpretability from an information-theoretic angle, building on ARC's mechanistic estimation agenda. This means accepting several changes to that agenda:

  • We only aim to explain networks trained and run with small Gaussian noise \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)\) on every activation, added after a normalisation. We expect a few bits of precision per dimension to be enough, possibly with somewhat wider layers.
  • An explanation is used for the tails: to give one-sided certified bounds on rare events, and to guide unbiased samplers that estimate them. It does not give better estimates of averages such as the loss.
  • Explanations must hold on everything they describe, including internal states the weights never produce.
  • Their cost is measured in bits: how much broader each description is than what the network actually does.

With this noise, a forward pass is a Markov chain whose transition densities are explicit Gaussians:

$$p(h_0,\dots,h_L)=\mathcal{D}(h_0)\prod_{l=1}^{L}\mathcal{N}\big(h_l;\,f_l(h_{l-1}),\,\sigma^2 I\big).$$

Importance weights for samplers over these paths are therefore exact. Each layer is also a Gaussian channel, so the information it carries is bounded and every quantity below can be stated in bits:

$$I(X;H_L)\le\dots\le I(X;H_l)\le C_l=\tfrac d2\log_2\!\big(1+P/\sigma^2\big).$$

2. What an explanation is used for

An explanation is a tractable description of each layer. Going forward, it is a distribution \(q_l\) from a family that can be pushed through the layer cheaply (sparse codes are our leading candidate). For estimation it would also include backward functions \(\psi_l\), described below. We see two uses, both about rare events. The forward part is the one we have worked out; the backward part is not yet developed.

Certified upper bounds on rare events

Choose \(q_0\) covering the input distribution, and each \(q_l\) covering the image \(K_lq_{l-1}\) of the previous one under the noisy layer, computed from the weights. Coverage carries over to the real activations, giving a one-sided guarantee for any output event \(A\):

$$D_\infty(p_L\,\|\,q_L)\le\varepsilon:=D_\infty(\mathcal{D}\,\|\,q_0)+\sum_l D_\infty(K_lq_{l-1}\,\|\,q_l),\qquad \Pr[f(x)\in A]\le 2^{\varepsilon}q_L(A).$$

The bound is tight when the descriptions put little mass on states that never occur. It is one-sided and does not estimate averages such as the loss.

Rare-event estimates below the sampling floor

With \(n\) samples, plain sampling resolves probabilities down to about \(1/n\). The zero-variance sampler draws each layer from the noisy transition reweighted by the probability of still reaching \(A\):

$$h_l\sim K_l(h_l\mid h_{l-1})\,v_l(h_l),\qquad v_l(h)=\Pr\big[A\mid h_l=h\big].$$

We have not yet worked out how to build a tractable approximation \(\psi_l\approx v_l\); the natural candidate is to propagate tractable descriptions backward from \(A\), mirroring the forward chain. Given any such \(\psi_l\), we get an unbiased estimator by twisted sequential Monte Carlo or multilevel splitting: draw from the real noisy layer and reweight by \(\psi_l(h_l)/\psi_{l-1}(h_{l-1})\). This needs only pointwise values of \(\psi_l\), not exact averages, and any positive \(\psi_l\) keeps the estimate unbiased; a better \(\psi_l\) lowers the variance. If \(\psi_l\) is within a constant factor of \(v_l\) at every layer, the relative variance grows linearly with depth under the usual stability conditions.

3. Relation to ARC's agenda

This builds on ARC's mechanistic estimation agenda and keeps its comparison with sampling at equal compute. The main differences are below; whether they pay off is open.

The restriction has costs: the noise may change what networks learn, tractable descriptions with little waste may not exist for trained networks, backward descriptions may approximate \(v_l\) too loosely to reduce variance much, and the approach says little about averages such as the loss.

4. How the costs might scale

We have two simple arguments, not yet checked in experiments, and two open questions. Throughout, \(d\) is the width, \(L\) the depth and \(b\) the number of bits of precision per dimension.

What the simple arguments give:

  • Depth. The noisy layers cannot increase divergences, so the excess of \(q_L\) over the real activations is at most the sum of the per-layer wastes, \(D_\infty(q_L\,\|\,p_L)\le\sum_l w_l\). If descriptions have a fixed size and are re-projected at every layer, total waste and compute therefore grow at most linearly with depth. This says nothing about whether each \(w_l\) can be made small.
  • Width and precision. A description that knows nothing beyond the noise has waste of order \(d\,b\) bits per layer, so waste never needs to exceed that. Such a description is useless for bounds; it only sets a ceiling.

What we do not know:

  • Whether descriptions with small waste exist for trained networks at a cost polynomial in width. In the worst case they do not: the size needed can grow exponentially with the information a layer carries, and such questions about networks are computationally hard in general. Like ARC, we are betting that trained networks have structure, sparse or factorised, that tractable families can capture.
  • How the quality and cost of the backward functions \(\psi_l\) scale. We have not analysed this.

5. First tests

Small transformers trained on synthetic worlds whose exact Bayesian answers and latent variables are known, so that the input distribution is exactly the prior and the quantities the network should compute are known in advance. We start with the forward chain: compare description families by waste and compute layer by layer, and test certified bounds on rare outputs against plain sampling at equal compute. Building backward functions \(\psi_l\) and testing guided estimation comes after.
