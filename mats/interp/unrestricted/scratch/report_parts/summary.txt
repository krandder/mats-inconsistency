## Summary for Kelvin

**Short answer.** The adaptive deductive estimator I built (a certified branch-and-bound) beats sampling only at
width 3, and its cost grows exponentially with width: that part of the progress prong failed. The difficulties prong
then found two things. First, "deterministic" is no restriction on random weights, so the question is whether
sampling is optimal among all algorithms, and no one can currently prove that. Second, the natural conjecture that
plain Monte Carlo is optimal is false: astra's red-team of my conjecture found a multilevel sampler that shares the
late layers through one reference trajectory and beats radius-exact MC by a factor that grows with depth (proven
asymptotically; measured 15x at width 8, depth 256; break-even at ARC's 256 x 32). So deterministic estimators can
beat plain sampling at depth, by better sampling rather than by deduction, and ARC's question at depth becomes
matching that sampler without sampling.

**Progress prong (MEASURED, pre-registered, 72 nets of widths 3-32 and depths 4-64).**
- The estimator is a branch-and-bound over input cones. It is exact on every cell where all units are certified
  stable, bounds every other cell with symbolic linear bounds computed from the actual downstream weights, returns a
  certificate, and refines where the certified gap (or a backward-sensitivity proxy) is largest.
- Against radius-exact scrambled-Sobol RQMC at equal FLOPs: [width 3]; [width 4]; [widths 6-8]; [widths 16-32]. The
  certificate is [result].
- Cost: [passes to a fixed precision against width and depth]. A cell costs 46-170 forward passes. Nothing in this
  family can reach 10M+ parameters.
- Unscrambled Sobol points do [about as well as] RQMC, but they are sampling at fixed points, and the gain of any
  such design over radius-exact MC is capped by the low-degree content of the net's angular function: the measured
  2-design ceiling at L = n/4 falls from about 7 (width 16) to about 2 (widths 64-256); about 4x for positive designs
  in the infinite-width limit (proven by astra).

**Difficulties prong.**
1. In an idealised arithmetic model, determinism is no restriction on random weights (PROVEN; one exact construction
   from the first layer's rotation symmetry, one from the weights' digits). So the question is whether sampling,
   implemented as well as possible, is optimal among all algorithms. "As well as possible" matters twice: batching
   inputs through fast matrix multiplication beats serial MC by n^{0.19}, and the multilevel sampler beats
   radius-exact MC by Omega(n / log^2 n) at depth proportional to width (PROVEN UNDER schoolbook-type costs; measured
   two-level gains 1.5x at L = n = 128, 3x at L = 4n, 7x at L = 16n, 15x at width 8 and depth 256; none at L <= n/4).
2. No one can currently prove that any estimator is optimal. It would be a super-linear time lower bound for a natural
   explicit problem, and I know of no technique for those. The worst-case barriers (IKW, #NSETH) do not reach random
   weights, the interpolation route to average-case hardness needs a polynomial estimand, and the known average-case
   hard problems (spin-glass partition functions, random-circuit amplitudes, permanents) concern exact or relative
   precision. ARC's mean is the additive, infinite-temperature end, which sampling already reaches.
3. Restricted classes that provably fail, with sizes: positive-weight designs (gain at most about 4 in the
   infinite-width limit); polynomials in the weights (the mean's Hermite degree grows with depth, about 2, 7 and 17 at
   depths 4, 16 and 32; plug-in estimates put degree-8 polynomials at a few to 17 MC samples at depths 16-32);
   first-layer cell selection (exponentially little mass); stable estimators (must be as rough as the mean). Any
   estimator must also meet three proven necessary conditions (correlation, final row, depth recursion).
4. Your analogies (section 4). Threshold-circuit derandomisation: close in the worst case (ReLU nets contain TC0), but
   on random weights its barrier disappears; no transfer to average-case hardness is established. Convex-body
   volume: the oracle separation has a proven ReLU analogue (no black-box algorithm beats MC by more than about 4x); no
   white-box or average-case reduction is established. Spin glasses: the only precise, testable observation; the
   infinite-width kernel fails the full-RSB concavity test from depth 9, which suggests, for a Gaussian surrogate
   only, an overlap gap for finding near-maximal outputs. It says nothing about means.
5. Bottleneck (section 5): the revised conjecture C0*, that nothing beats the best multilevel sampler by a
   polynomially growing factor at depth proportional to width. It has little evidence; its cheapest refutation is a
   better control for the first layers' amplitude variation, which carries the variance the reference trajectory
   cannot remove.

**Which prong wins.** For deductive estimators the difficulties prong wins: none of ours or of the analysed classes
matches sampling at scale, and no impossibility proof is in reach. The one positive result of the lane came from
red-teaming the difficulties prong: plain MC is the wrong baseline at depth.
