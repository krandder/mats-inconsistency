# Where does the agent's model come from, and what does that process minimise? (Friston)

Kelvin's question, verbatim: "what is his [Friston's] explanation about where the agent's model comes from and what does *that process* minimizes?"
2026-10-03, from texts fetched and read that day; printed page numbers; "(unverified)" = not read in a primary source.

**Short answer.** Friston says the model comes from three nested processes (evolution, development, learning) and that all three minimise *the same* variational free energy, only on slower timescales. At the evolutionary level, though, "free energy" is just a new name for (negative) fitness: whatever persists counts as having a low-free-energy model. The question is pushed back to natural selection, and selection has no objective beyond "what survived, survived". Friston calls this tautology "deliberate". It is a definition plus an analogy, not a theorem.

## 1. The three levels and what each is said to minimise

Friston, Thornton & Clark 2012 ("Free-energy minimization and the dark-room problem", Front. Psychol. 3:130) is the paper where he answers Kelvin's objection directly. Clark plays the philosopher and asks: "is not part of what we need to explain the origin of that very agent, whose features and properties help determine what will count as surprise?" Friston replies: "the free-energy principle provides the mapping from the space of all possible models ... to a single model that has the greatest chance of survival ... If you specified a particular environment, you could use the free-energy principle to specify its optimum inhabitant. This is because your specification of the environment is an implicit generative model and that model will have the lowest free energy of all models." And: "a dark room will afford low levels of surprise if, and only if, the agent has been optimized by evolution (or neurodevelopment) to predict and inhabit it."

| Level | Process | What it is said to minimise | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evolution | Natural selection treated as Bayesian model selection: the phenotype is the model | Free energy, by analogy with model evidence. Equivalently it maximises "free fitness", and "adaptive fitness and (negative) free energy are considered by some to be the same thing" | Life as we know it (2013), sec. 4: "at even longer timescales, evolution can be cast in terms of free energy minimization—by analogy with Bayesian model selection based on variational free energy [Friston & Penny 2011]"; FTC 2012 |
| Development / structure learning | Growing or pruning structure by Bayesian model comparison; Bayesian model reduction (BMR) scores reduced priors from one fitted model ("a metaphor for sleep", p. 145) | Free energy (bound on log evidence: complexity minus accuracy) | Book Box 7.3, p. 147: "would my free energy increase or decrease if I were to (for example) eliminate elements of a likelihood matrix?"; Friston, Parr & Zeidman 2018 |
| Within-life learning | Parameter updates (Dirichlet counts; Hebbian plasticity) | Variational free energy | Life as we know it, sec. 4: "optimizing the parameters of a generative model ... such that they minimize free energy"; book sec. 7.5 |

Preferences, the part that matters for the dark room, are placed at the top level: "These preferred states are first and foremost defined by niche-specific evolutionary adaptations. However ... in advanced organisms these can also extend to learned cognitive goals" (book p. 42). And: "in Active Inference, the identity of an agent is isomorphic with its priors" (p. 39). Friston 2013 (BBS 36:212-213, commentary on Clark), as quoted by Klein 2018 (manuscript p. 6): "where do these prior beliefs come from? They come from evolution and experience, in the sense that if we did not have these prior beliefs, we would be drawn to dark rooms and die there" (unverified; read only as Klein quotes it).

The physics papers do not *select* a model at all; the model is defined as the system's own statistics. Friston 2019 (p. 89): "the generative model (i.e., NESS density)"; p. 101: the particle is "guaranteed to use the correct generative model, because it has already been selected through the emergence of the Markov blanket". Friston et al. 2023: cost "is the Lagrangian or surprisal of sensory paths, which defines the characteristic trajectories of a particle". Preferences are, by definition, the thing's typical paths.

Friston also admits, in his own words, that the origin of the priors is outside the argument. Life as we know it, sec. 4 (last paragraph): "this paper has only considered inference as emergent property of self-organization—not the nature of implicit (prior) beliefs that underlie inference."

## 2. Theorem, definition, or hypothesis?

The claim "existence implies the model encodes preferences that make persistence low free energy" splits into three parts:

- **Definition (the core).** The "model" is the steady-state (or path) density of whatever persists, and "preference" means high probability under that density. So "persisting things prefer their persistent states" holds by construction. Friston agrees: "Dark-Room agents can only exist if they can exist. The tautology here is deliberate, it appeals to exactly the same tautology in natural selection ... it is just a characterization of biological systems that exist" (FTC 2012). Book Box 6.2 (p. 117), via the complete class theorem: "asking whether a behavior is (Bayes) optimal is meaningless."
- **Conditional mathematics, partly broken.** "Blanket at NESS implies internal states minimise free energy" is meant as a lemma. Biehl, Pollock & Kanai 2021 (Entropy; arXiv 2001.06408, abstract and sec. 8) "prove by counterexample that the original free energy lemma, when taken at face value, is wrong". Three of the six steps in the 2013 argument "do not hold". Even where the lemma holds, it re-describes the flow; it does not pick the model.
- **Hypothesis / analogy (evolution).** "Evolution minimises free energy" is offered "by analogy" (2013) with a citation to Sella & Hirsh's "free fitness" (FTC 2012). No source I read derives it. (Replicator dynamics having the form of Bayes' rule with fitness as likelihood is a recollection, unverified, not cited in these texts.)

The critics agree on this reading. Klein 2018 (sec. 3, published p. 2552): "Appeal to apparent tautologies should trouble you. For whatever tautologies do, they don't explain why things happen." Sun & Firestone 2020 (TiCS 24:346-348): "If leaving a dark room is explained by predictions that we will not linger in dark rooms, then no behavior could be inconsistent with PP." Colombo & Wright 2021 (sec. 4.1) call its status "muddled" and note that a "provable definition of living systems" is not provable (fn. 12). The defenders effectively concede that the model is an input. Van de Cruys, Friston & Clark 2020 (reply to Sun & Firestone): "Once we define an organism and niche, designating initial bodily ... structure and capabilities, and thereby seeding them with initial expectations about the kinds of state they should inhabit ... everything else must follow."

## 3. Is there a parameter-free tie-breaker against both rooms?

Expected free energy (book eq. 2.6, p. 33) = risk (KL between predicted outcomes and preferred outcomes C) + ambiguity (expected entropy of P(o|x)). Equivalently, it is minus (information gain about hidden states) minus (pragmatic value).

- **Defined over** the agent's own hidden states (and, as "novelty", its parameters, eq. B.34): expected KL from prior to posterior, relative to the model's state space, not the world.
- **With preferences removed (flat C over outcomes),** my own algebra from eq. 2.6 gives risk = −H[Q(o)] + const. So G = H[o|x] − H[o] = −I(x; o) under the agent's predictive model. In a dark room I = 0. In a white-noise room I = 0 *if the model puts the noise in the likelihood* (ambiguity): ambiguity is high, and the book says active inference "avoids getting into such situations ... given their low potential for information gain" (p. 34). A structured room has I > 0, so it wins with no scalar to tune. This is the best parameter-free result in the sources. But the 2023 paper ("if we ignore preferences ... expected free energy reduces to expected information gain") and the book (p. 36) only state the reduction; they do not draw this conclusion about the two rooms.
- **The catch** (my analysis, not stated in the sources). If the model represents the noise as fresh hidden states that the screen reveals exactly (the "noisy TV"), the information gain is maximal at every step, and the agent sits in front of the noise. Noise-in-likelihood versus noise-as-state is a structural choice, made in Friston's scheme by structure learning over a given model space. Parameter novelty fades for i.i.d. noise as counts grow (like learning progress), on the same structural choice.
- **Acknowledged free parameters.** Tschantz, Seth & Buckley 2020 (Discussion, "Active inference"): "we introduced an artificial weighting term to keep the epistemic and instrumental value within the same range. The same effect could have been achieved by constructing the shape (i.e. variance) of the prior preferences P(o)". In their Methods, eq. 18, δ "is set to 1/10". In the canonical form there is no explicit weight, but the scale (precision) of C and policy precision γ do the same job.

## 4. Verdict for Kelvin

Friston: the model comes from evolution, development and learning, each minimising free energy. At the bottom, free energy *means* improbability of the system's own characteristic states, so evolution "minimises" it only because survivors are, by definition, those whose states stayed probable: fitness renamed. Your objection stands. The FEP does not say which preferences a system should have; it labels the survivors' preferences as their model. The non-circular content is how a given model is updated (BMR, parameter learning) and the information-gain term, which beats both rooms only once the model's split between hidden states and noise is fixed: the free parameter moves into model structure. On the "no fitted parameters" bar, these sources fall short. Their closest candidate is the flat-preference reduction to I(x; o), worth keeping as a conditional result.
