Loss as the inconsistency of what you believe

1. The idea in one paragraph

Write down everything you believe as a pile of probability statements, each with a confidence, and allow the pile to contradict itself. Then ask: what is the cheapest way to make it consistent? Pick one single joint distribution, and charge each statement for how far that distribution is from what the statement claimed, weighted by its confidence. The smallest possible total charge is the pile's inconsistency. Richardson's point is that the standard training losses are this number for the obvious pile: cross-entropy is the inconsistency of “my model says p” plus “the data is what it is (certain)”; L2 regularisation is a Gaussian prior you trust with confidence β; the VAE's negative ELBO is the inconsistency of encoder, decoder, prior and one image. So you never have to pick a loss: pick the beliefs, and the loss is how much they disagree. In market language, which is ours rather than the paper's: each statement is a quoted price, the confidence is how much money stands behind the quote, and the inconsistency is what an arbitrageur can take from those quotes risk-free.

2. What a PDG is

A probabilistic dependency graph (Richardson & Halpern 2021) is a set of variables and a set of arrows. Each arrow carries a claim, “given its source, the target is distributed like this”, plus a confidence β. Unlike a Bayesian network, two arrows may point at the same variable and say different things. That is the whole trick: a PDG can hold a contradiction, and it can say how big the contradiction is. paper, Def. 1

An arrow with no source is a plain claim (“P(rain) = 0.3”). An arrow with β = ∞ is a hard constraint, for example a logical definition. An arrow with β = 0 is ignored.

Worked example: three sentences about two facts

Variables A (“it rains”), B (“the grass is wet”) and C (“A and B”). Four arrows:

  • P(A) = 0.4, P(B) = 0.7, P(C) = 0.5, each with confidence 1 (three price quotes);
  • a two-tailed arrow A, B → C that says “C is exactly A AND B”, with confidence ∞ (the logic).

Picture: circles are variables, arrows are claims. The double-headed orange arrow is a function (deterministic, infinite confidence), drawn the way the paper draws functions.

Is this consistent? No, and you can see it in your head: “A and B” can only be true when A is, so P(C) ≤ P(A), but the quotes say 0.5 > 0.4. A trader sells C at 0.5 and buys A at 0.4: ten cents in, and in every world A pays at least what C costs, so he keeps at least ten cents. That free money is the contradiction.

What the PDG does with it. Look for the single joint distribution over the four worlds (rain yes/no × wet yes/no) that the three quotes, weighted by confidence, mind least. The answer is P(A) = P(C) = 0.449, P(B) = 0.700, with world “rain, not wet” at probability 0; the leftover charge, the inconsistency, is 0.0102 nats. fleet math, R5 computed here The repair is what you'd guess: the bound C ≤ A becomes tight, A and C meet at 0.449, the average of their log-odds (logit 0.4 = −0.41, logit 0.5 = 0, average −0.20, back to 0.449), and B, which nobody disputed, stays put.

The two formulas, in words

For a candidate joint distribution μ, each arrow X → Y sends a bill: β times the extra nats you'd pay encoding μ's Y-values (given X) with that arrow's code instead of μ's own. The total is the incompatibility of μ:

Inc(μ) = Σ over arrows β · E_{x~μ} KL( μ(Y|x) ‖ p(Y|x) ) [paper eq. (1)] ⟨⟨M⟩⟩ = min over μ of Inc(μ) [the inconsistency]

An infinite-confidence arrow sends an infinite bill unless μ obeys it exactly, so β = ∞ arrows are constraints. Adding arrows or raising confidences can only raise the inconsistency (Lemma 1, “believing more can't make you less inconsistent”); most of the paper's proofs are this one lemma. paper, eq. (1), Lemma 1

There is a second, qualitative score (information deficiency, weighted by α) that breaks ties among the cheapest μ. The paper says it plays only a minor role there. In the γ → 0 limit it picks a unique best μ (Richardson & Halpern 2021, Prop. 3.3-3.4); for our logic PDGs that tie-break is the maximum-entropy distribution with the repaired prices. paper, §2 fleet math, R2(b)

3. The inconsistency by hand: two quotes on one coin

The smallest inconsistent PDG: one variable, a coin, and two arrows into it. Alice says P(heads) = 0.9, Bob says P(heads) = 0.1, each with confidence 1.

We want the one coin, P(heads) = m, that minimises KL(m‖0.9) + KL(m‖0.1). The trick that makes it doable in your head: a sum of KLs to p and q, with weights r and s, equals (r+s) times a KL to the normalised geometric mean of p and q, minus a constant. So the best coin is that geometric mean, and the constant is the answer. derived here; the closed form for the value is the paper's Lemma 10

geometric mean, heads: √(0.9 × 0.1) = 0.3 geometric mean, tails: √(0.1 × 0.9) = 0.3 normaliser Z = 0.3 + 0.3 = 0.6 → best coin m* = 0.3/0.6 = 0.5 inconsistency = −(r+s)·ln Z = −2·ln 0.6 = 2 × 0.511 = 1.022 nats check directly: KL(0.5‖0.9) = 0.5·ln(0.5/0.9) + 0.5·ln(0.5/0.1) = 0.511 KL(0.5‖0.1) = 0.511 (mirror image) sum = 1.022 ✓

Rule of thumb you can use anywhere: the compromise odds are the confidence-weighted geometric mean of the quoted odds. 9:1 and 1:9 at equal confidence give 1:1. 4:1 and 1:1 give 2:1, so P = 2/3, and the inconsistency is −2·ln(√0.4 + √0.1) = 0.105 nats. With Alice three times as confident, 9:1 and 1:9 give 93/4·9−1/4 = 3:1, so P = 0.75. computed here

The same number as money

Now let each quote be a market maker using Hanson's log market scoring rule, with liquidity equal to its confidence. The makers do not know they are quoting the same coin. An arbitrageur sells heads to Alice and buys heads from Bob, the same number of shares from each, so whatever the coin does he is flat. He stops when both quote the same price.

shares each side: ln 9 ≈ 2.20 (both prices then sit at 0.50) received from Alice: −ln(0.1 + 0.9/9) = −ln 0.2 = 1.609 paid to Bob: ln(0.9 + 0.1·9) = ln 1.8 = 0.588 risk-free profit: 1.609 − 0.588 = 1.022 = the inconsistency ✓

This is not a coincidence. For any belief table, the inconsistency equals the largest risk-free profit one trader can take from per-quote LMSR makers, and his trades leave the prices exactly at the PDG's repaired table. fleet math, R4 (Fenchel duality); checked numerically on 15 logic instances and on four asymmetric coin cases here One caveat: against quotes that do not move when traded (fixed prices), the free money is a different number. In the rain example a fixed-price trader makes 0.10 per share while the inconsistency is 0.0102; roughly, inconsistency ≈ (distance to coherence)², and it is the price impact of the LMSR that makes the two equal. fleet math, R5

At β → 0 Alice is ignored: the coin is Bob's and the inconsistency is 0. At β → ∞ the coin is Alice's and the inconsistency rises to KL(Alice‖Bob), which is the paper's Prop. 9: “certain of p, confidence β in q” gives β·KL(p‖q). Every value in between is a scaled Rényi divergence (Cor. 10.1). The compromise price moves along the confidence-weighted average of the two log-odds.

4. Which loss is which inconsistency

Every row is a proposition in the paper, checked against the text. “Data at ∞” means the empirical distribution is held with infinite confidence: you are not willing to move it.

Section 8 of the paper adds a practical case: training on corrupted data d and a simulator s. Mixing the two datasets ignores their redundancy; weighting by the product makes h overconfident (h* ∝ s² when d = s); trusting d with λd and s with λs gives the λ-weighted geometric mean, which is calibrated. This is widget 2's compromise rule, applied to a predictor.

5. Why this matters for the logic network

The goal (BRIEF-MATH): a network whose repeated blocks are thinking time. Given at block 0 a small propositional theory and some query sentences, with no new facts and no adversary, its prices should drift toward what a logical inductor would say. The paper gives us the fixed point to drift toward. Here is how the pieces map, with what our own math notes added.

The belief state as a PDG

  • Atoms are variables; each query sentence is a variable defined from the atoms by an ∞-confidence arrow (its truth table), exactly like C = A∧B above.
  • Axioms are ∞-confidence arrows saying “this clause is true”. Together they forbid every world that breaks an axiom.
  • The network's price table is one finite-confidence arrow per sentence, “P(φ) = p”, with confidence β = how much logic must pay to move that price (the sentence's liquidity).

Then the inconsistency is: over all mixtures of axiom-respecting worlds, the smallest confidence-weighted log-score distance to the price table. fleet math, R1 Three consequences, all proved in our notes:

  1. Zero exactly when the prices are fair. The inconsistency is 0 if and only if some distribution over models of the axioms reproduces every price, which is the same as “no portfolio wins in every world”, i.e. de Finetti coherence, i.e. LI's one-day affine coherence. R3
  2. It is the arbitrage on offer to relation traders. The traders who bet on identities (“A∧B ≤ A”, “P(A) + P(¬A) = 1”) can take at most the inconsistency, in one shot, from LMSR makers who ignore the axioms; their best trade moves prices to the repaired table. R4 No history and no adversarial market maker: the “arbitrageur” is just the other half of the optimisation (its dual variable).
  3. Minimising it is the coherent projection. The repaired table q* is unique, every identity holds, entailed sentences go to 1 and refuted ones to 0 even if their input price was 0.01, and each undecided price moves in log-odds space by (the arbitrageur's position) / (its confidence). R2 It also beats the raw table in log score in every possible world, by at least the inconsistency. R4, dominance

What coherence cannot do: the house view

Every coherent table has inconsistency 0, so the projection has no opinion among them. Given axioms only and no prices, “P(A) = 0.01” and “P(A) = 0.99” are equally perfect if A is undecided. LI wants something specific there (roughly, the frequency of A across possible worlds). The fix is one more arrow: a house view, a prior over whole worlds, held with some confidence. The fixed point then is the house view restricted to the possible worlds: entailed 1, refuted 0, and anything else the house view's share of the possible worlds where it holds. Learning the house view from data by cross-entropy is, by Prop. 3, another inconsistency of the same PDG, so fitting and reasoning are one objective. frequency-and-prior; IDEAS §1 This also matches Richardson & Halpern's remark that conditioning is resolving the inconsistency created by adding an observation arrow (2021, §5). pdg2021

The block it suggests

Our design is a market of world-holders: N seats, each holding one complete world. A sentence's price is the share of seats whose world makes it true, averaged over blocks. Every seat's world is checked against the axioms, so the prices are coherent at every block, not just in the limit. Each block, a learned proposer suggests moves to nearby worlds, an exact clerk rejects any that break an axiom, and a Metropolis coin accepts moves so that, over many blocks, worlds are held in proportion to the house view. The fixed point is the PDG answer for house view + axioms (+ optionally the block-0 intuition table at finite confidence). Thinking time has one job: finding worlds. IDEAS §2 The variant closest to the paper is a Frank-Wolfe block: each step the arbitrageur names the possible world his bets lose most in, and the prices mix toward it; this reaches the projection at about 1/t and certifies its own distance each block. R6

What the paper does not give

  • Frequencies. Inconsistency is blind among coherent tables; the house view must come from somewhere else (data, or a design choice).
  • Timeliness. The paper is static: one number for one belief state. Nothing in it says how fast a block should approach the projection, or that a theorem should be priced near 1 before its derivation finishes (LI's provability induction). That has to be built or trained in.
  • Computation. The paper never asks how hard the minimisation is. For logic it is hard: deciding whether a table has inconsistency 0 is probabilistic SAT, NP-complete. Up to about 12-16 atoms a block can list the worlds; beyond that it must search. R6 the NP-completeness citation (Georgakopoulos et al. 1988) is recollection
  • Per-trader guarantees and paradoxes. A fixed point protects only the traders' sum. LI's “no single efficient trader profits forever” and its paradox resistance need wealth carried across blocks, which a single inconsistency number does not track. IDEAS §2 table
  • The money reading itself. Inconsistency as LMSR arbitrage is our result, not the paper's. The paper reads it as coding cost (“extra bits”).

6. The three sharpest open questions

  1. Coherent now, or honest now? When worlds cannot be listed, the world-holder market is coherent at every block but prices a sentence at 1 as soon as every world found so far makes it true, without a proof. LI makes the opposite trade (never certain without proof, coherent only in the limit). How much overconfidence remains after N found worlds, and what is the right price between the “inside” bound (found worlds) and the “outside” bound (derived rules)? No math yet. IDEAS §5, item 2
  2. Does block 20 control block 500? A free block trained with a loss only at block 20 can hide a clock: our counterexample has error 0 at block 20 and 0.86 at 500, and a proven solver given a learned step size and momentum reached 1035 at block 500. Capped steps plus a loss at a random block from 1 to 20 is the proposed fix; it is proved for contractions and only conjectured for the templates we want to train. block-dynamics R9 conjecture
  3. Whose model? The paper's motto is “choose your model, not your loss”. Here the model is the confidences (per-sentence liquidity) and the house view, and both must be learned. Does the seat market still converge when the house view is tilted by the block-0 intuition table and that tilt is updated as it goes, and does a trained net's price sequence over depth behave like a martingale, as the ideal predictor's would? IDEAS §5, items 5 and 8 conjecture
